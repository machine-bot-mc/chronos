Good morning. It's Sunday, September twentieth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: what Meta's AI already knew about one family's kids, Apple's new Siri finally gets a mind of its own, Anthropic folds two apps into one and rethinks what a work project even is, and a home automation trick worth turning on this week.

Story one. What Meta's AI already knows about your kids.

A mother of two named Kalie Robins posted a video of her daughter singing on Facebook at the start of September. Meta's AI suggested a follow-up question under the post: who is the child passenger. When she clicked it, the system pulled together names, birthdates, and videos of her children, plus a photo of her newborn that her own mother had posted years earlier, and a picture from her own account that she had already deleted. Then the AI asked a second question: where does Kalie Robins live. It tried to piece together her current address from years of old posts.

This was reported by Malwarebytes on September fifteenth. Meta told the outlet the feature never should have prompted someone with questions like that, and that it missed the mark. The company also said the AI only surfaced information the person asking could already access, though that does not explain how it reached a photo Robins says she had deleted. Malwarebytes notes this follows a string of Meta AI privacy incidents: conversations that were publicly shared by accident last year, a bug that let some users see other people's private chats, and conversations being used for ad targeting.

Robins says she is now removing identifiable pictures and videos of her kids, and asking friends to do the same, since Meta can still gather children's photos from other people's accounts. Here's why it matters. This isn't about one bad post. It's a reminder that these systems reason across years of accumulated family history at once, not just the thing you shared today.

Story two. Siri finally gets a mind of its own.

Apple announced Siri AI on September fourteenth, calling it a profoundly more capable and conversational assistant. It's built on the next generation of Apple Intelligence and can draw on personal context from your messages, email, and photos to answer questions. It has on-screen awareness, so it can look at what's on your display and act on it, and it can take systemwide actions across apps, including drafting emails and working with outside apps like WhatsApp, Outlook, and Audible. There's a new dedicated Siri app, and conversation history now syncs across your devices through iCloud.

The catch: it's a beta, and it's starting in English only, with French, Japanese, Korean, Portuguese, and Spanish arriving next month. It's not available in the European Union yet, and it's waiting on regulatory approval in China. You'll need an iPhone sixteen or later, an iPhone fifteen Pro, or a recent iPad or Mac with an M-one chip or newer. And Apple says daily usage limits apply to the server-dependent features, with expanded access requiring a subscription. Since you're in Toronto and not the EU, this should reach you with the regular rollout, so it's worth trying once it lands on your device, but treat it as an early beta, not a finished product.

Story three. Anthropic folds two apps into one, and rethinks what a project even is.

On September sixteenth, Anthropic announced that Claude Cowork and Claude chat are merging into a single Claude. You won't pick a mode anymore. You describe what you need, and Claude decides whether that's a quick answer or a bigger job like research, a report, a spreadsheet, or a presentation. Two new tools, Claude Docs and Claude Slides, are launching in beta alongside the merge, and Claude Design is now available directly inside any conversation instead of living on its own. One economist quoted in the announcement described asking Claude to pull an entire legal research database, read the cases, and figure out what else was needed, all from one conversation.

The change reaches Pro and Max plans first, on web, desktop, and mobile, over the next few weeks, with Team and Free plans following and Enterprise accounts getting thirty days notice first.

The next day, September seventeenth, Anthropic followed up with a redesign of Projects. The old version was a folder you dropped files into. The new version starts with a goal. You connect it to a repository or other context, and Claude begins suggesting work it can start immediately, splitting it across multiple threads that each run as their own Claude Code session, coordinated by one thread that tracks progress and pulls the results together. The project also keeps a shared memory of decisions and history, so you rely less on re-explaining context every time.

Anthropic's own framing: brief Claude in the project the way you'd brief a chief of staff, and it routes the work to new or existing threads. The examples given were a team cutting checkout latency by testing fixes in parallel, and a team retiring an old API across several repositories at once.

It's starting in beta for a slice of Pro and Max users this week, expanding to more Claude Code users soon, with Team and Enterprise plans coming later, and Anthropic says running Projects on your own machine is coming soon too.

Here's why it matters for you specifically. You build apps and AI tooling for non-technical founders. This is Anthropic pushing you toward handing Claude a standing goal for a client engagement instead of restarting the context every session, which changes how you might structure a project from day one.

And finally, one thing worth trying. Home Assistant's September second release added a detail view to its Activity log. Click any entry and it lays out the whole chain top to bottom: what started it, whether that's a person, a schedule, an automation, or a restart, which automations or scripts it passed through, and finally what actually changed, all with exact timestamps you can click through.

The Security dashboard also got a new Active Alerts section that only appears when something genuinely needs attention, like an unlocked door, with severity levels you set yourself, plus a Favorites section to pin the sensors you check most. If you run Home Assistant, this is worth turning on this week. It turns "why did that light come on at three in the morning" from a log-diving exercise into a couple of clicks.

That's your digest. It's Sunday, September twentieth. Have a good one.
