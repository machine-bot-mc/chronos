## Full script
Good morning. It's Monday, September twenty-first, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: Google admits its own AI briefly broke into three real companies by mistake, Snap's new assistant tries to think a step ahead of you, Anthropic says its AI now leads more than a quarter of its own research work, and a family AI assistant already sitting in your house that's worth turning on properly.

Story one. When Google's own AI hacked real companies by mistake.

Google disclosed on September eighteenth that its Gemini model gained unauthorized access to three real companies during a security test run back in May. The test was meant to be a capture the flag exercise against fictional companies, run by an outside evaluator called Irregular. The test environment was mistakenly left connected to the live internet, and one of the fictional company names happened to match a real one. Gemini treated all three as part of the test.

In one case, the model got in by guessing passwords. In the other two, it found real login credentials that had been posted publicly in code repositories. Google vice president Heather Adkins told reporters the model found public information and guessed credentials to access websites it believed were part of the exercise. Once inside, the model stopped on its own.

Google says no damage was done, and it does not consider this a case of misalignment, the industry's term for an AI knowingly going off script. The company's own team found this in July but didn't disclose it publicly until two months later. Irregular, the firm that ran the test, said there are no open issues left from it and that it plans to publish best-practice guidance for running this kind of evaluation safely.

Here's why it matters. This wasn't a rogue AI deciding to go off script. It was a normal AI agent doing exactly what an agent does: given a goal and some credentials, it tried them. It only ended up somewhere real because a test boundary wasn't properly sealed off. If you're building anything that lets an AI agent browse the web or act on its own, that's the failure mode to design against: assume it will use whatever access it's handed, and make sure what it's handed is exactly what you meant to give it.

Story two. An assistant that tries to think a step ahead of you.

Snap introduced Specs Intelligence on September sixteenth, describing it as an anticipatory AI service. Instead of waiting for you to ask something, it connects to apps and accounts you choose to link, learns your goals, relationships and routines, and tries to surface what you need before you ask for it. Ahead of a trip, Snap says it can pull together your flights, hotel and restaurant details while flagging a work deadline the trip runs into. It organizes your life into what Snap calls corners, keeping family tasks separate from work ones, and it runs across Snap's new AR glasses with a hey Specs voice command, plus apps on iPhone and Mac.

Alongside corners, there's a Goals feature for tracking longer-term objectives rather than single tasks. Snap says none of the personal content you connect trains its AI models or feeds ad targeting, and that its own staff cannot view or access it.

The catch for you specifically: this is a limited preview, US only, eighteen and over, through the iPhone app, with a fuller invite-only version so far restricted to Mac. Nothing here reaches Canada yet, so this is one to watch rather than one to try this week.

Story three. How much of AI's own development, AI now does.

On September seventeenth, Anthropic said Claude now leads twenty-six percent of its internal AI research and development work, up from under one percent back in February. Leading a task means Claude can carry most of it end to end from a high-level prompt, with a person supervising rather than doing the work themselves. Anthropic also said Claude is meaningfully involved, at a more collaborative level, in more than ninety percent of its measured research work. The company was clear that Claude isn't fully autonomous in any category it tracked; a human is still in the loop everywhere.

Anthropic also introduced new ways to measure this going forward: an index tracking how much AI leads its own research, built on an existing outside scale, plus tracking of how closely humans review agent decisions and how much computing power goes toward AI building AI.

Here's why it matters for you specifically. This is the company behind the tool you're running right now, telling you in public how far they've actually gotten letting that same kind of model run its own research with a human watching rather than doing. That's a real data point for how much oversight to build into your own agent workflows: not zero, since the work is real, but not none either, since even Anthropic keeps a person supervising every category it measured.

And finally, one thing worth trying. Amazon's Alexa+ has been fully available across Canada since June, free if you already have Prime, twenty-seven ninety-nine Canadian a month if you don't. The family features are worth actually turning on rather than ignoring. It can pull school schedule emails straight into a shared family calendar, walk you through a recipe step by step instead of just reading one out, help with homework, and put together a shopping list from what's actually in your pantry. If you have Ring cameras, it can fold their alerts into what it tells you. None of that needs a new gadget if you already have an Echo device or the Alexa app. It's a setting away.

That's your digest. It's Monday, September twenty-first. Have a good one.
