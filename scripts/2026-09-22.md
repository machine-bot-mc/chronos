Story one. Meta's AI bet pays off, for now.

Meta's stock climbed about twenty-five percent this month, including a single-day jump of about seven percent, its best month in more than two years. That rally is tied to Muse, the personal AI agent Meta launched on September eighth. Muse doesn't just answer questions, it acts inside your connected accounts: sending email, booking travel, filling out forms, and making purchases, with your approval required on sensitive steps. It reached the number one spot on the United States Apple App Store's free apps chart within its first week, ahead of ChatGPT. This was reported on September twenty-first. Wells Fargo raised its price target on Meta to seven hundred ninety-six dollars, and Wells Fargo, Goldman Sachs, and Morgan Stanley all called Muse a meaningful inflection point in Meta's AI strategy. Mark Zuckerberg has framed the whole push as personal superintelligence: agents that do the task, not just talk about it.

Here's why it matters. Muse is free to start, with paid tiers at twenty dollars and one hundred dollars a month for heavier use. But it's US only for now, and for users eighteen and up, so if you were hoping to hand it your inbox from Toronto, that's not on the table yet. Worth watching for if and when it crosses the border.

Story two. A security flaw hit the tool you use to build things.

Researchers at Air Security disclosed a bug they're calling Plugin four shell, affecting four major AI coding agents: Claude Code, OpenAI's Codex, GitHub Copilot, and Google's Gemini CLI. It lets an attacker who controls a plugin repository sneak in altered code while the tool still reports that its security check passed. The trick abuses how git handles a branch name that matches a commit's ID: the agent ends up checking out the attacker's branch instead of the exact, reviewed version it believes it pinned to. No click and no approval is needed, and it can trigger again automatically the next time the agent updates its plugins in the background, which is the default behavior in both Claude Code and Codex.

The researchers found this back in May and privately told all four companies in June. Anthropic patched Claude Code in mid-June. OpenAI patched Codex in August. Microsoft has not shipped a fix for GitHub Copilot, and Google has not fixed Gemini CLI at all, telling users to move to a different product instead.

Here's why it matters for you specifically. You build apps and AI tooling, which likely means coding agents with plugins installed right now. If your Claude Code or Codex has been keeping itself updated, this one's already closed. If you or your team run Copilot or Gemini CLI with third-party plugins, there is currently no fix, so that's worth a second look this week.

Story three. Your AI agent can now pick up the phone for you.

On back to back days last week, both of the leading consumer AI agents added the ability to make real phone calls. On September sixteenth, Meta's Muse gained the ability to call businesses on a user's behalf, though only US businesses, rolling out first to people who had specifically asked for it. The very next day, a smaller rival called Instinct launched its own calling feature, named Concierge, aimed at what its founder called high touch cases: booking a restaurant that doesn't take online reservations, getting onto a dentist's cancellation list, or arguing down a cable bill. It's in early access, expanding over time.

Instinct is moving fast on funding too. It's reportedly seeking one billion dollars at a ten billion dollar valuation, up from two and a quarter billion just the month before, with more than one hundred thousand users on an invite-only app. Its founder said users have told the company they've planned cross-country road trips, bought their weekly groceries, and cancelled hundreds of dollars of subscriptions through it.

Here's why it matters. Both of these calling features are US-only and both are early access. But the direction is clear: within a year or two, calling a business to sort something out may increasingly be a task you hand off rather than do yourself. Worth tracking for when either one opens up to Canadian numbers.

And finally, one thing worth trying: an app called Toki that turns forwarded emails into calendar events automatically. Forward it a flight confirmation, a hotel booking, or a meeting invite, and it pulls out the details and creates the event without you typing anything. It also takes voice notes and screenshots, and it syncs with Google, Outlook, and Apple calendars. If your calendar chaos mostly comes from confirmation emails you never get around to entering by hand, this is a five minute setup that pays for itself the first time you forward something instead of typing it in.
