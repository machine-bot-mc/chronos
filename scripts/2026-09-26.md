## Full script
Good morning. It's Saturday, September twenty-sixth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: fascinating facts. Three hearts, blue blood, a brain spread through its arms, and a nursery more than three kilometers under the Pacific. Today it's all about octopuses.

Today's theme: octopuses, and the strange biology that makes them like nothing else in the ocean.

Fact one. Three hearts, and one of them shuts off to swim. An octopus has three hearts. Two of them exist just to push blood past the gills, where it picks up oxygen. The third heart takes that oxygen-rich blood and pumps it out to the muscles and organs. Here's the strange part. When an octopus swims, that third heart actually stops beating. Smithsonian Magazine reports that this helps explain why octopuses would rather crawl across the seafloor on their arms than swim, since swimming quickly exhausts them. The same blood is unusual too. Human blood is red because it carries oxygen using an iron-based protein called hemoglobin. Octopus blood is blue, because it carries oxygen using a copper-based protein called hemocyanin instead. Smithsonian Magazine reports that hemocyanin is a bigger molecule, and it actually moves oxygen more efficiently in the kind of extreme environment octopuses live in, where deep water runs cold and oxygen is scarce. The tradeoff, according to the same article, is that blood built this way is extremely sensitive to changes in acidity, which matters as ocean chemistry keeps shifting. That's all from a Smithsonian Magazine article most recently updated in March twenty twenty-four.

Fact two. Most of its brain isn't in its head. About two-thirds of an octopus's neurons sit out in its arms, not in its head. Smithsonian Magazine describes what that makes possible. Some arms can work out how to open a shellfish while the rest of the octopus is off doing something else entirely. Even a severed arm will still jerk and react on its own for a while, because so much of the processing that controls it never lived in the head to begin with. It's less one brain giving orders than eight semi-independent ones, loosely coordinated.

Fact three. Camouflage faster than a blink. National Geographic reports that octopuses can change their color and texture faster than the human eye can follow, calling it the swiftest known change in the animal kingdom. The trick is in their skin, which is covered in tiny pigment-filled sacs called chromatophores. Each one sits inside a ring of muscle. When the muscle stretches, the sac floods with color. When it contracts, the color hides again, and together, millions of these sacs create a moving picture across the octopus's body. Michael Vecchione, curator of cephalopods at the Smithsonian's National Museum of Natural History, told National Geographic, quote, they're the best at it of anything that we know, end quote. There's a real mystery buried in this. Most octopuses are color-blind, so scientists still don't fully understand how an animal that can't see color knows exactly what color to become. That article was published in May twenty twenty-four.

Fact four. A nursery more than three kilometers down. Researchers first spotted this site in October twenty eighteen, when a team from NOAA's Monterey Bay National Marine Sanctuary and the Ocean Exploration Trust, working aboard the research vessel Nautilus, found thousands of octopus clustered around a rocky ridge near Davidson Seamount, an extinct underwater volcano off the coast of California. MBARI kept studying the site for years afterward, sending the remotely operated vehicle Doc Ricketts down on fourteen separate dives between twenty nineteen and twenty twenty-two, and running time-lapse cameras that captured more than twelve thousand images of the seafloor over several months. In twenty twenty-three, MBARI published the results in the journal Science Advances. They counted more than five thousand seven hundred pearl octopus nesting in one small patch of seafloor there, with an estimated twenty thousand or more across the full site. Only three other deep-sea octopus nurseries like it are known anywhere in the world, two off central California and two off Costa Rica, and this one is the largest by far. The surrounding water sits at just one point six degrees Celsius, barely above freezing. But cracks in the rock leak warm water from underground, reaching nearly eleven degrees, and female pearl octopus tuck their eggs specifically into those warm cracks. In water as cold as the surroundings, the researchers had expected the eggs to take five to eight years, or longer, to develop. Inside the warm cracks, they hatched in under two years instead. The mothers stay there the entire time, shielding their eggs and living off their own reserves, and die once the eggs finally hatch. MBARI senior scientist Jim Barry, who led the research, put it simply. Quote, the deep sea is one of the most challenging environments on Earth, yet animals have evolved clever ways to cope, end quote.

So the same body that makes an octopus so strange up close, three hearts, blue blood, a mind spread out through its arms, skin that outpaces its own eyesight, is also what lets a mother more than three kilometers down find the one crack in the seafloor that runs just warm enough to give her eggs a chance.

That's your digest. It's Saturday, September twenty-sixth. Have a good one.
