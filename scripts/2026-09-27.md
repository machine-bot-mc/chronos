## Full script
Good morning. It's Sunday, September twenty-seventh, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: book reviews. Two titles this week, a deeply reported look inside OpenAI, and a new novel from a Pulitzer winner that hits shelves in two days.

Today's first book is Empire of AI, Dreams and Nightmares in Sam Altman's OpenAI, by the journalist Karen Hao. It came out in May of twenty twenty-five, from Penguin Press. Hao started covering OpenAI back in twenty nineteen, and says she originally thought they were the good guys, a nonprofit built around AI safety, meant to be a check on more purely profit-driven labs. The book traces how that mission changed. It draws on interviews with people inside the company, reporting on data workers in Kenya, water activists in Chile, and the inside story of Sam Altman's brief firing and return as CEO in late twenty twenty-three.

Critics were largely impressed. Tim Wu, reviewing it for The New York Times, called it excellent and deeply reported. In The New Yorker, Benjamin Wallace-Wells wrote that Hao's reporting inside OpenAI is exceptional, and persuasive on the labor and environmental implications. The Economist called it deeply researched and gripping. The Financial Times described it as timely and myth-busting, well reported, and said it doesn't pull any punches.

Writing in The Irish Times in July, under the headline Precise, insightful, troubling, the critic Karlin Lillington called it a meticulously detailed profile of the company and its controversial leader. She said it's convincing on why we shouldn't let companies like OpenAI and Microsoft make certain decisions for us alone. Then, this past March, the National Book Critics Circle gave Empire of AI their Nonfiction award for twenty twenty-five. The judges' citation put it plainly: the book shows how altruistic ideas turned into a naked quest for dominance, reliant on networks of exploitative labor.

One thing worth knowing before you pick it up. In December, Hao published a correction on her own website. A line in chapter twelve had compared the water use of a proposed Google data center near Santiago to the nearby town of Cerrillos, and suggested the data center would use over a thousand times what the town needed in a year. That figure traced back to a government document that had mislabeled its units, litres instead of cubic metres. The corrected version says the data center could use about as much water in a year as the town's entire population of eighty-eight thousand people, not a thousand times that. Hao acknowledged the mistake publicly once readers flagged it, and fixed the text.

It's a reminder that even a well-reviewed, heavily reported book can carry a source's bad math into print, and that the dramatic number often travels further than its quiet correction does.

The title itself points at something bigger than one company. Hao frames OpenAI's rise through the lens of nineteenth century colonial empires, and reviewers have compared her approach to the historian William Dalrymple's writing on the East India Company. OpenAI itself declined to cooperate with the book, and Sam Altman publicly criticized it after it came out. The reporting behind it is unusually deep for the genre, drawing on roughly two hundred sixty interviews, and this year the book also picked up the New York Public Library's Helen Bernstein Award for excellence in journalism.

Who's it for. If you want the on-the-record version of how one of the most consequential companies of the decade actually operates, not the version in its own press releases, this is that book. If you build with AI tools for a living, it works as a corrective to the hype, less about what these systems might someday do, and more about the very human, messy decisions being made behind them right now.

Book two. Ply, by Hernan Diaz, comes out this Tuesday, September twenty-ninth, from Penguin Random House. It's four hundred and sixty-four pages, and it's Diaz's first novel since Trust, which won the twenty twenty-three Pulitzer Prize for fiction. Trust told its story of a secretive financier and his wife through four conflicting fictional texts, a novel, an autobiography, a memoir, and a diary, and left readers to sort out which version of events to believe.

Ply is set centuries in the future, in a version of New York after the collapse of the federal government. It follows an orphaned young woman with a rare talent for illegally pulling electricity from the grid to sell on the black market. A physicist recruits her into an experiment that could rewrite how reality itself works, while she's still searching for something like the family she lost.

Advance reviews are strong. Kirkus gave it a starred review, calling it a brainy, challenging, and engrossing trek into a battered but unbeaten future. Library Journal also starred it, writing that Diaz brilliantly envisions how love, trust, and loyalty can remain. Vogue called it a page-turning, pleasurably disorienting, and deeply humane vision. Writing for Shelf Awareness, the critic Harvey Freedenberg said Diaz never loses sight of the pincher's personal drama and her palpable ache to be restored to something akin to the family she once knew, and called the novel unquestionably the product of a fertile imaginative intelligence.

Who's it for. If you liked the puzzle-box structure of Trust, or you just want literary fiction that takes technology and power as seriously as it takes character, advance word says this one is a pre-order, not a wait and see.

And finally, one thing worth trying. With the OpenAI book especially, read it alongside the author's own corrections page rather than just the first printing. It's a short read, and a rare chance to watch a working journalist show her math after the fact.

That's your digest. It's Sunday, September twenty-seventh. Have a good one.
