## Full script
Good morning. It's Tuesday, September twenty-ninth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: business.

Three stories from the last few months. The sale of Moneris, one of Canada's biggest payment processors. A used-car auction deal south of the border. And where the Bank of Canada stands after its September decision.

Story one. Moneris changes hands.

On August eleventh, Moneris announced that it is being acquired by the private equity firm Francisco Partners. According to the company's announcement, the cash price is about two billion Canadian dollars. Moneris has been owned jointly by the Bank of Montreal and the Royal Bank of Canada, and each bank receives fifty percent of the proceeds.

Here is what Moneris is. The announcement says it has operated for more than twenty-five years and processes one in three transactions across Canada, through more than three hundred and twenty-five thousand points of commerce. It employs nearly two thousand people, with its headquarters in Toronto.

There are conditions. The deal needs approval under the Retail Payment Activities Act and the Competition Act. Closing is expected at the end of the first quarter of the banks' fiscal year twenty twenty-seven. Long-term referral agreements will also be set up with both BMO and RBC, so the banks keep a commercial link to the business they are selling.

Why it matters. Two of the country's largest banks are stepping back from owning the machinery behind everyday card payments, and a private equity buyer is stepping in. The announcement does not say what will change for merchants, so that is unknown for now. If you or your clients take card payments in Canada, this is worth watching, because the regulators' review will be the first signal of how the deal is judged.

Story two. Copart buys ACV.

On September tenth, Copart announced a definitive agreement to acquire ACV Auctions for ten dollars and fifty cents a share in cash. That values ACV's equity at about one point nine billion dollars. According to the announcement filed with the United States Securities and Exchange Commission, the price is a premium of about forty-five percent to ACV's closing stock price on August tenth, before the deal was public.

Copart describes itself as a global leader in online vehicle auctions, with operations at more than two hundred and fifty locations in eleven countries. ACV is a digital marketplace for used vehicles, serving dealers and commercial clients. The companies say the combination creates, quote, a full-spectrum, digital vehicle remarketing platform, end quote, covering dealer trade-ins, wholesale, salvage and international resale.

The deal is structured as a tender offer. It needs a majority of ACV's shares to be tendered, and it needs the waiting period under the American antitrust law known as Hart-Scott-Rodino to expire or end. The companies expect it to close by the end of this year.

Why it matters. This is a big established player buying its way into the dealer-to-dealer auction channel, rather than building that channel itself. It is a United States deal, and the filings we read do not give a Canadian angle. For a builder, it is one more sign that online marketplaces for physical goods are consolidating.

Story three. The Bank of Canada holds, with a warning.

On September second, the Bank of Canada left its policy rate at two and a quarter percent. That is a hold, and according to a TD Bank article, it is the seventh in a row.

The Bank's statement gives the backdrop. The economy grew three point three percent in the second quarter. Unemployment was six point four percent in July. Inflation is around three percent, but about two point two percent when gasoline is excluded.

Now the caveats. Quote: "new US tariffs and Canadian counter-measures have been announced following the breakdown of trade talks between Canada and the United States." End quote. The Bank said these could raise costs for some businesses and feed into consumer prices over time. It also said that the continuing conflict in the Middle East is keeping energy prices high, and that the longer high oil prices persist, the greater the risk of spillover to other prices.

Its next decision is October twenty-eighth. TD economist Marc Ercolao expects the Bank to hold the rate through the rest of the year. Forecasts from other banks were not something I could confirm, so I am leaving them out.

Why it matters. Borrowing costs have been steady for a while, but the Bank itself says the risks to inflation are tilted upward. Businesses with variable-rate debt, or with imported inputs hit by tariffs, should not assume the calm continues.

And finally, one thing worth trying. If you advise founders who sell in Canada, ask them one question this week: what happens to their costs if tariffs and interest rates both go up? Most have never written the answer down.

That's your digest. It's Tuesday, September twenty-ninth. Have a good one.
