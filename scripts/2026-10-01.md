## Full script
Good morning. It's Thursday, October first, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: personal AI. Three stories from the past few days: an always-on agent from OpenAI, a phone for kids that keeps AI on a short leash, and Apple's reported plan to put Siri on a screen in your kitchen.

Story one. OpenAI launches Dots.

On Tuesday, September twenty-ninth, at its DevDay event, OpenAI launched Dots. TechCrunch reported it that day. A dot is a personal agent inside ChatGPT, powered by a model called GPT-6 Astra. TechCrunch describes dots as meant to operate, quote: "independent of any specific hardware or interface, pursuing user-defined goals continuously in the background with minimal oversight." End quote.

Here is what that means in practice. According to a DataCamp explainer, each dot gets its own cloud computer and browser. It keeps working after you close the chat. You can reach it through ChatGPT, Slack or Teams, and a limited texting beta. DataCamp says the design targets small, recurring jobs that were never worth building automation for. Invoice reminders. A weekly calendar review. Batched bug-fix notes.

Now the limits. DataCamp says you set rules for each dot, with four options: act on its own, act if pre-approved, ask first, or escalate to you. Sensitive tasks like password changes stay with you. A dot cannot call you, and it has no email address of its own. You can only create one from the desktop app or a desktop browser, not from your phone. And deleting what a dot has remembered means deleting the whole dot.

On access, dots are rolling out to Pro and Business Premium subscribers in eligible markets. For Pro, the launch leaves out the European Economic Area, Switzerland and the United Kingdom. The sources I read did not say whether Canada is included, so check before you plan around it. Your first dot is included in those plans, and the first month of usage does not count against your allowance. What happens after that has not been published.

Why it matters. Last week the story was assistants that answer. This week it is assistants that keep working while you are away. For anyone building tools for non-technical founders, the interesting part is the permission model. The product is really a set of dials for how much trust you hand over.

Story two. Freckle, a phone for kids with no app store.

Freckle is a New York City startup, founded by Sam Terris, and it announced a phone for children aged seven to thirteen. Engadget covered it on September thirtieth. The phone costs one hundred ninety-nine dollars, plus twenty-five dollars a month for service. The company's own site says the first batch of units ship in December twenty twenty-six, and it is taking deposits now.

The phone runs a simplified system called ClayOS. There is no app store, no open web, no streaming video and no social media. Calls and texts go only to contacts a parent approves. It has a small three-inch screen, a walkie-talkie mode between Freckle devices, a mini map, and tap to pay.

The AI is deliberately narrow. Engadget lists image recognition and question answering, and nothing like an open chatbot. Parents can also create custom quests for their kids, and the parental app lets them unlock features over time.

The limits. Engadget says it is best suited to walkable places like New York City. I did not find anything on whether it will be sold or supported in Canada. And these are launch claims. No one has reviewed a finished unit that I could find.

Why it matters. Most family AI news is about adding more. This is a product where the pitch is taking things away, and using AI only for a few jobs a parent can understand.

Story three. Apple's smart home push.

MacRumors reported on September thirtieth, citing Bloomberg's Mark Gurman, that Apple plans to announce three home products on Tuesday, October thirteenth. They are a smart home hub, a new HomePod mini, and a new Apple TV. All three are expected to show off a more capable Siri. MacRumors notes the hub was originally planned for twenty twenty-four and was delayed to wait for that Siri technology.

The report also mentions later products. A larger HomePod. A home security camera that senses where people are in a room without recording video. And a premium hub with a robotic arm display.

The caveat is simple. This is a report, not an Apple announcement. Prices, features and Canadian availability are not in what I read. Treat October thirteenth as a date to watch.

Why it matters. If you already live in Apple's world, this is where your home and your assistant may finally meet. Wait for the real announcement before buying anything.

And finally, one thing worth trying. If you already run a smart home and want it to explain itself, Home Assistant's September release, published on September second, adds an Activity page. It shows the full chain of events behind a change. What triggered it, which automations ran, and what changed. It also adds a map of how your Matter devices connect over Thread and Wi-Fi. That is not new this week, but it is a calm way to see why your house did something odd.
