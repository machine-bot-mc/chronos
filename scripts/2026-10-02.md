## Full script
Good morning. It's Friday, October second, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: international news.

Three stories from the past few days. Russia's nuclear warning to NATO over Kaliningrad. The last American troops leaving Iraq. And explosions in the Ethiopian capital, Addis Ababa.

Story one. Russia's Kaliningrad warning.

On Wednesday, September thirtieth, Russia sent a diplomatic note to NATO member states. According to Al Jazeera, the note says Moscow has, quote: "information that NATO is preparing an air and naval blockade of Kaliningrad." End quote. Kaliningrad is a Russian enclave on the Baltic Sea, wedged between Poland and Lithuania. Both are NATO members.

Reuters, which saw the note, reported that Russia said it would be ready to use, quote: "the entire arsenal of forces and capabilities at its disposal, including nuclear weapons." End quote. That would be if the alliance tried to cut the enclave off. The note also threatened strikes on decision-making centers in NATO countries from the outset of any conflict.

NATO's Secretary General, Mark Rutte, answered that NATO is a defensive alliance. He said Russia should, quote: "stop the nuclear threats." End quote. He called the note unhelpful.

Here is the limit on what we know. Al Jazeera notes there is no confirmation that NATO is preparing any blockade. The only source for that claim is Russia itself.

Why it matters. Nuclear language in a formal diplomatic note is a deliberate signal. Al Jazeera puts it against a backdrop of recent tension in the region, including alleged airspace violations and a Russian warship firing flares near a Danish military helicopter. It is a warning to watch, not a sign that any blockade is planned.

Story two. The last American troops leave Iraq.

The Pentagon said on Wednesday that the withdrawal of American troops from Iraq is complete. The Associated Press reported that the final troops left an air base in Irbil, in Iraq's Kurdish region. That ends a twelve-year mission against the Islamic State group, which began in twenty fourteen. The exit follows an agreement between Baghdad and Washington. Al Jazeera says the plan was set in twenty twenty-four.

Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell called it an orderly departure. Quote: "This milestone reflects the success of a twelve-year campaign that defeated ISIS as an organized military threat." End quote. President Trump called the wider American involvement in Iraq, quote: "a very expensive excursion into Hell." End quote.

Iraq's prime minister, Ali al-Zaidi, described it as the start of a new phase based on Iraqi sovereignty.

Not everyone is celebrating, for different reasons. The Kurdish regional government's Peshmerga Ministry voiced concern about losing American air defenses. It said the region faced, quote: "more than one thousand drone and ballistic-missile attacks" End quote, during the recent conflict between the United States and Iran. On the other side, a Kataib Hezbollah official, Abu Mujahid al-Assaf, said the departure of what he called occupation forces was the beginning of full sovereignty. A group calling itself the Islamic Resistance in Iraq said it is not bound by any timeline over its weapons.

Why it matters. Iraq now has to manage security without American troops on its soil. Al Jazeera notes the Islamic State is territorially defeated but still has cells in the region. Kurdish officials fear exposure to Iranian strikes.

Story three. Explosions in Addis Ababa.

Reuters reported on Thursday that explosions were heard overnight in Ethiopia's capital. Three diplomatic sources confirmed this. A source linked to the opposition alliance said it had carried out several drone strikes in the city, including some aimed at the military's headquarters.

Reuters could not verify that claim. Witnesses said there was no visible damage near the military base. The prime minister's office, the federal government and military spokespeople did not immediately respond to requests for comment.

If the claim is true, it would be the first known drone attack by anti-government forces on the capital. The opposition was not previously known to operate armed drones. On Wednesday, the national intelligence service had announced an indefinite ban on drone flights over Addis Ababa and the surrounding area.

Why it matters. Last week's fighting in the north was the biggest escalation in Ethiopia since the civil war of twenty twenty to twenty twenty-two ended. The capital is hundreds of kilometres from the front lines in Tigray, Amhara and Afar. Anxiety about renewed war is growing, and what actually happened overnight is still unclear.

And finally, a note on reading these stories. In each one today, a key claim came from a single interested party. Russia on the blockade. An opposition source on the drones. When that happens, the careful move is to wait for confirmation.
