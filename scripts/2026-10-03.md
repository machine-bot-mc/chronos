## Full script
Good morning. It's Saturday, October third, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: fascinating facts. Our theme is the Moon, our closest neighbor, and six things about it that are stranger than they look from the sidewalk.

Today's theme: the Moon. Most of these facts come from NASA's Moon pages, which were last updated on February twelfth, twenty twenty-six. Some supporting details come from the encyclopedia Wikipedia, and I will say so when they do.

Fact one. The Moon is small, and it is slowly leaving. NASA says the Moon's radius is about one thousand eighty miles, or one thousand seven hundred forty kilometers. Here is NASA's picture of it. Quote: "If Earth were the size of a nickel, the Moon would be about as big as a coffee bean." End quote. NASA puts the distance at about two hundred thirty-nine thousand miles, or three hundred eighty-five thousand kilometers. And it says the Moon is getting about an inch farther away each year. That sounds like nothing. But Wikipedia's entry on the Moon says that in five billion years, the Moon will have wandered forty percent further away from Earth than it is now.

Fact two. The Moon swings between roasting and freezing. NASA says the sunlit surface reaches about two hundred sixty degrees Fahrenheit, or one hundred twenty-seven degrees Celsius. In shadowed regions, it drops to about minus two hundred eighty degrees Fahrenheit, or minus one hundred seventy-three degrees Celsius. By my arithmetic, that is a gap of three hundred degrees Celsius between the hottest and coldest figures NASA gives. That is a very big swing for one world.

Fact three. The Moon has quakes, and they can last. NASA's moonquake page describes several kinds. Deep moonquakes happen hundreds of miles beneath the surface, and NASA calls them tidal events, caused by Earth's gravity. Others come from the Moon cooling. Thermal moonquakes come from the surface temperature changing between lunar day and night. And meteoroid impacts shake the Moon too. NASA says the cooling quakes can register up to five point five on the Richter scale, and can last for over ten minutes. We know all this because Apollo astronauts left seismometers on the surface. The Apollo eleven mission carried a seismic experiment with four seismometers, powered by two panels of solar cells.

Fact four. The Moon is probably the product of a collision. NASA says the Moon likely formed after a Mars-sized body collided with Earth several billion years ago. It adds that within about one hundred million years, most of the molten surface had crystallized. Wikipedia names that impactor Theia, and dates the Moon's formation to four point five one billion years ago, not long after Earth itself formed. Notice the word likely. This is the leading explanation, and the source does not call it settled.

Fact five. The Moon has two different kinds of month. According to Wikipedia, the Moon takes twenty-seven days, seven hours and forty-three minutes to orbit Earth, measured against the stars. But the time from one new moon to the next is twenty-nine days, twelve hours and forty-four minutes. Wikipedia also explains that Earth's tides pulled the Moon into a lock, so it always shows us the same near side. That is why we only ever see one face from Earth.

Fact six. The ground is dust, and the air is almost nothing. NASA says nearly the entire Moon is covered by a rubble pile of charcoal-gray, powdery dust and rocky debris called the lunar regolith. It says the Moon has a very thin and tenuous atmosphere called an exosphere, and that it is not breathable. Gravity on the surface is one-sixth of Earth's. NASA also says today the Moon has a very weak magnetic field, and that Earth's is many thousands of times stronger. And one landmark to picture: NASA says the Tycho Crater is more than fifty-two miles, or eighty-five kilometers, wide.

And finally, one thing worth knowing. We have brought pieces of the Moon home. NASA says Apollo astronauts returned a total of eight hundred forty-two pounds, or three hundred eighty-two kilograms, of lunar rocks and soil. NASA also says that more than one hundred five robotic spacecraft have been launched to explore the Moon. It adds that the Moon is the only celestial body beyond Earth, so far, that has been visited by human beings. That is worth sitting with for a moment. So the next time you look up, you are looking at a world that people have studied for decades, and that is still drifting away an inch a year.

That's your digest. It's Saturday, October third. Have a good one.
