## Full script
Good morning. It's Sunday, October fourth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: book reviews.

We have two books from this year. A novel set on a Scottish island, and a true story about a death in London. For each, I'll tell you what it's about and what named critics said.

Book one. John of John, by Douglas Stuart.

John of John was published on May fifth, twenty twenty-six, by Grove Press. It runs about four hundred pages. According to Wikipedia, it is a finalist for the twenty twenty-six Orwell Prize for Political Fiction, was shortlisted for the twenty twenty-six Booker Prize, and was picked for Oprah's Book Club.

The setting is the Isle of Harris, in the Outer Hebrides, in the late nineteen nineties. Cal MacLeod is twenty-two. He has finished art school in Edinburgh and goes home to his family's croft. His father, John, is a sheep farmer, a weaver and a Presbyterian deacon. The book explores family secrets, a strained bond between father and son, and the gap between their values. The title comes from a traditional Scottish naming practice, which the book explains as Cal crosses the stretch of water called The Minch. I'm keeping the plot to that, so nothing is spoiled.

Now the critics. As summarized on Wikipedia, Mark Harris in the New York Times Book Review wrote that Stuart builds something Quote: "different, stranger and far more original." End quote. Hamilton Cain in the Boston Globe wrote that the Quote: "prose is gorgeous and his plotting strategic." End quote. Publishers Weekly praised the Quote: "deeply humane character work." End quote.

Booklist's reviewer, Juen Sawyers, wrote that the Quote: "prose is a joy to read and get lost in." End quote. That is the common thread in the praise. It is about the language as much as the story.

Not everyone agreed. Sam Sacks, in the Wall Street Journal, found the central men to be grown men who merely behave like children, which made their struggles harder to embrace. And Michael Arditti, in The Spectator, questioned whether John is believable, given his strict Calvinist beliefs.

So who would enjoy it? Readers who like quiet, place-driven family fiction, and who don't mind a slow reveal. If you want a fast plot, the doubters have a point.

Book two. London Falling, by Patrick Radden Keefe.

London Falling was published on April seventh, twenty twenty-six, by Doubleday. It's about three hundred eighty-four pages. Keefe is the author of Say Nothing and Empire of Pain, and like those books, this one grew out of a New Yorker article.

According to the publisher's page, it examines the death of nineteen-year-old Zac Brettler, who fell from a luxury tower beside the River Thames in November twenty nineteen. His parents then uncovered a secret about his life, and connections to London's criminal underworld. The publisher says the book was named a New York Times Best Book of the Year so far, and is a finalist for the twenty twenty-six Kirkus Prize for nonfiction and a shortlisted title for the Baillie Gifford Prize.

The reviews were strong. The review aggregator Book Marks counted twenty-eight raves, six positives, one mixed and one pan, at the time I checked. Here is a sample from that site.

Andrew O'Hagan, in the London Review of Books, wrote that Quote: "his reporting has the texture of fiction." End quote. Mark O'Connell, in the New York Review of Books, called it Quote: "Infernally gripping" End quote, and Quote: "A brilliantly crafted book." End quote. Ian Thomson, in The Guardian, on April seventh, wrote Quote: "scrupulously researched" End quote, and Quote: "Grimly absorbing." End quote.

Jennifer Szalai, in the New York Times Book Review on April eighth, called Keefe a Quote: "master builder of intricate narratives." End quote. Frank Langfitt, on NPR, described him as a master at using true crime as a vehicle for exploring society. Laura Miller, in Slate, wrote that the best true crime Quote: "use[s] particular event as key to unlock a world." End quote.

For a Canadian angle, Ian Brown of the Globe and Mail called it the Quote: "most intimate true story" End quote he has read from Keefe, and a Quote: "gripping human story." End quote.

Tobias Grey, in Air Mail, called it a Quote: "profound exploration of parental grief." End quote. And Noah McCormack, in the New Republic, described it as a Quote: "powerful indictment of British justice system." End quote.

There was a dissent. Philip Hensher in The Spectator wrote that the Quote: "story is little more than brutal anecdote." End quote. That is the one pan the aggregator listed.

Who would enjoy it? Fans of Say Nothing and of true crime that opens up a whole social world. It deals with grief and a young man's death, so it can be heavy.

And finally, one thing worth trying. Both books have strong reviews on both sides. Before you pick one, read one praising review and the dissenting one. It takes ten minutes, and it tells you quickly which kind of reader you are.

That's your digest. It's Sunday, October fourth. Have a good one.
