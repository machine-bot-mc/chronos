## Full script
Good morning. It's Thursday, October eighth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: personal AI.

Three stories. A new text-message assistant called Tab comes out of stealth. A startup called Underdog launches an assistant that runs entirely on your own computer. And a TechCrunch roundup shows how crowded the field of family assistants has become.

Story one. Tab comes out of stealth.

TechCrunch reported on October seventh that Tab, a personal AI assistant, officially launched on Tuesday, October sixth. The report puts the company's valuation at three hundred million dollars. The company declined to share exact funding details. Named investors are SV Angel, Valar Ventures and American Spirit.

Tab works by text, over iMessage or WhatsApp. It handles everyday jobs like ordering groceries, finding gifts, booking things and paying bills. One of the founders, Ammar Amdani, said it has its own phone number, computer and wallet. He described the idea this way. Quote: "You text it like you'd text someone you trust and it takes the weight off." End quote.

On privacy, Amdani said that user data is never used to train AI. He said card details are held in a vault that Tab cannot see, and that nothing irreversible happens without the user's approval. Those are the company's own claims. TechCrunch did not verify them independently.

What we don't know is just as useful. The article gives no pricing and no user numbers. Amdani said growth is mostly word of mouth, and that a new product is coming soon.

Here's why it matters. An assistant with its own wallet is a different kind of product from a chatbot. The approval step is the design choice to watch. For anyone building tools for non-technical founders, the question is how much a person will let software spend without asking.

Story two. Underdog keeps it all on your machine.

TechCrunch reported on October sixth that a startup called Conway Research launched an invite-only beta of Underdog on Monday. It is an assistant that runs entirely on the user's own device, so the data stays on hardware they already own.

Right now it works on Macs and Windows PCs. Linux, iPhone and Android versions are listed as coming soon. The model is a twenty-seven billion parameter reasoning model, fine-tuned from an open model. Founder Sigil Wen says it compares favorably with a recent Claude model on some benchmarks. TechCrunch did not verify that. The article also notes that these models are much smaller than the ones hosted in data centers.

The pricing is unusual. It is free at first and never supported by ads. There is no subscription. Instead, Underdog would take a small percentage of payments the assistant makes through Stripe, similar to a card fee. That model is described, but it has not been proven at scale. The article does not say how much money the company has raised. Named investors include Andreessen Horowitz, Khosla Ventures and the Anthology Fund, a partnership between Menlo Ventures and Anthropic.

Here's why it matters. Most assistants this week send your messages to someone else's servers. Underdog is a bet that people will trade some raw power for keeping everything at home. It is invite-only and the privacy claims are the founder's own, so treat it as one to watch, not one to trust yet.

Story three. The family assistant crowd.

On October third, TechCrunch published a roundup of eighteen AI agents that live in your text messages. Several are aimed squarely at families.

Fambot calls itself a chief of staff for families. It covers school messages, sports, meal planning and calendars, and it sends a nightly summary of the next day. You can reply by text. It has been in beta since early September, with three and a half million dollars of pre-seed funding. It is free during the beta, and the company expects to eventually charge about what a Netflix subscription costs.

Ollie launched in June. It pulls together school messages, calendars, tasks and group chats. It has a free tier, then twenty-five dollars a month for one hundred fifty messages, or one hundred dollars a month for a thousand. The roundup says it is one of the first mainstream family assistants with SOC 2 compliance, which is an independent security audit standard.

Ohai turns messages, forwarded emails or voice requests into schedules, reminders and plans. It has a free basic option, and family subscriptions start at nine dollars and ninety-nine cents a month.

The roundup does not say where each of these is available, so I can't tell you which work in Canada. Check before you sign up.

Here's why it matters. The pattern is the same across all of them. They read the messy inbox of family life and hand back one plain daily plan. The prices already vary by a factor of four, so the market has not settled.

And finally, one thing worth trying. Pick one boring recurring chore, like the school newsletter, and ask yourself whether a nightly text summary would save you ten minutes. If yes, try a free tier of one family assistant for a week. Keep your most sensitive accounts out of it until you've read what it stores.

That's your digest. It's Thursday, October eighth. Have a good one.
