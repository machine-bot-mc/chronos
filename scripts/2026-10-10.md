## Full script
Good morning. It's Saturday, October tenth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: fascinating facts.

Today's theme is the honey bee. Four facts and a bonus, each traced to a source I'll name as we go.

Fact one. The price of a pound of honey.

A page published by NBC News on April fourth, twenty oh seven, relays a fact list from the Texas A and M University Department of Entomology. It says a forager must visit about two million flowers to make one pound of honey. It also says the average forager makes about one twelfth of a teaspoon of honey in her entire lifetime.

Put those together. A jar of honey on your shelf is the lifetime output of a very large crowd of bees. The same page says an average hive holds around fifty thousand bees. Note that the page is nearly twenty years old, and these are averages, not exact counts. But they give a sense of scale.

The same page lists a few more numbers. It says bees have five eyes, two pairs of wings and six legs. It says they fly at about twenty miles an hour. And it says bees have been around for about thirty million years.

The page also puts a value on their work. It says bees pollinate about one hundred thirty fruit, vegetable, nut, ornamental and fiber crops in the United States. It says that adds roughly fifteen billion dollars a year through higher yields and better quality. Those are American figures from two thousand seven, so treat them as a snapshot, not a current price tag. The page also says Americans eat an average of one point three pounds of honey per year. That is a lot of flowers.

Fact two. A dance that carries directions.

When a forager finds good food, she can go back to the hive and tell the others where it is. According to Wikipedia's article on the waggle dance, she does this on the vertical surface of the honeycomb. She runs forward while waggling her body.

The angle of that run encodes direction. A run pointing straight up means the food lies toward the sun. A run tilted to the right of vertical means the food lies that same angle to the right of the sun. The length of the waggle run encodes distance. The farther away the target, the longer the waggle.

The article gives an example. A run forty-five degrees to the right of vertical points to food forty-five degrees to the right of the sun.

Fact three. The man who decoded it.

The Austrian scientist Karl von Frisch was one of the first to translate the meaning of the waggle dance. Wikipedia's article on von Frisch says he shared the nineteen seventy-three Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Konrad Lorenz and Nikolaas Tinbergen. It says the citation named his pioneering work on communication between insects.

One caution. The Wikipedia page on the dance does not give a year for his decoding, so I won't guess one. What the sources do support is that the dance carries direction and distance, and that its decoder won a Nobel Prize.

Fact four. A defence made of heat.

The Asian giant hornet is a fearsome predator of bees. But a study in the journal Zoological Science, published in twenty twelve by Sugahara, Nishimura and Sakamoto, looked at how Japanese honeybees fight back. The bees swarm a hornet and form a tight ball around it.

Inside that ball, temperature, carbon dioxide and humidity rise quickly. The hornet dies within about ten minutes. The researchers exposed hornets to different humidity and carbon dioxide levels. They found that high carbon dioxide lowered the temperature at which hornets died. That suggests the bees' heat and their own breath work together.

The authors also note that Japanese honeybees can kill hornets without dying themselves. European honeybees, they say, are usually killed in that situation. This was a lab study of one species pairing, so it says little about other bees.

Fact five, a bonus. The winter furnace.

In an October first, twenty sixteen column for the American Bee Journal, Wyatt Mangum describes how a colony survives winter. When the outside temperature drops to about fifty-seven degrees Fahrenheit, the bees form a cluster.

The bees inside make heat by, quote: "micro-shivering their flight muscles without moving their wings." End quote. The article says the heat output is comparable to a twenty to forty watt bulb.

Where there is brood, the inside of the cluster is kept between ninety and ninety-seven degrees Fahrenheit. Without brood it is cooler, but stays above about sixty-four degrees. The bees on the outer shell face inward and act as insulation. That shell is kept a little above fifty degrees. A few degrees colder and those bees can fall into a chill coma and die.

And finally, one thing worth trying. Next time you pour honey, think of that one twelfth of a teaspoon. It makes the spoonful feel like a small gift.

That's your digest. It's Saturday, October tenth. Have a good one.
