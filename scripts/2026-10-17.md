## Full script
Good morning. It's Saturday, October seventeenth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: fascinating facts, with one theme. Time itself.

Three facts today. A missed train that reshaped the world's clocks. The most accurate clock reported in its day. And a quiet fact about Earth's own day, which is slowly growing longer.

Fact one. A missed train.

Smithsonian Magazine, in an article dated November eighteenth, twenty eleven, tells this story. Quote: "In eighteen seventy-six, Canadian railway engineer Sandford Fleming missed a train." End quote. He had read the railroad's timetable and assumed a departure was in the afternoon when it was in the morning.

Before railways, towns and cities set their time by local noon, when the sun is highest. That worked on foot. On a railway it was chaos, and the article says engineers, stationmasters, conductors and passengers all struggled with the differing local times.

Fleming's first idea was a single twenty-four hour schedule he called Cosmic Time, based on a theoretical clock at the center of the Earth. He then added twenty-four lettered local time zones, each fifteen degrees of longitude wide and one hour apart from its neighbor. Around eighteen eighty he had a watch made with local time on one side and Cosmic Time on the other. Smithsonian says it is held by the American History Museum.

Fleming was chief engineer of Canada's Pacific Railroad. The article says his railway role helped push through the eighteen eighty-three standardization of North American time zones. Smithsonian reports that the United States and Canada established five standardized North American time zones on November eighteenth, eighteen eighty-three. In eighteen eighty-four, over twenty countries met in Washington, and most agreed on a system, with Greenwich, England accepted as the zero degree meridian.

Notice what did not happen. Cosmic Time itself was never adopted. The zones were. That is all this one article says, so I will not claim more about who else deserves credit.

Fact two. A clock that would not slip for thirty-three billion years.

Inside Science, from the American Institute of Physics, reported on August seventh, twenty nineteen, that a clock built at the National Institute of Standards and Technology in Boulder, Colorado had laid claim to the title of world's most accurate. It is an optical atomic clock built around a single positively charged aluminum ion. The ion is held in electromagnetic fields and cooled to near absolute zero. It absorbs one very specific frequency of ultraviolet light, and a laser tuned to that frequency oscillates more than a quadrillion times per second. Those oscillations are the ticks.

Quote: "It would take thirty-three billion years for the new timekeeper to gain or lose a mere second." End quote. That is eleven billion years better than the previous record, set in twenty eighteen by another NIST clock that uses thousands of ytterbium atoms. The article says the best optical clocks are about one hundred times more accurate than the cesium microwave clocks that currently keep the world's standard time.

What are they for? Physicists use clocks like this to test whether fundamental constants shift over time. A shift could point to a hidden dark matter particle or new physics. The article also says the scientific community plans to use optical clocks to redefine the second in roughly the next decade, as of its writing in twenty nineteen.

Now the caveats. The title was only for now. NIST physicist Sam Brewer predicted the record would hold only briefly, since NIST was already developing a new aluminum ion clock likely to beat it. So I cannot tell you whether this record still stands today. The results were published in Physical Review Letters in July twenty nineteen.

Fact three. Your days are getting longer.

EarthDate, a science program, in an episode dated July tenth, twenty twenty, says that Earth's days are getting longer by about one point eight milliseconds per century. A millisecond is a thousandth of a second. So you will not notice it by tomorrow morning.

How do we know? Quote: "Even cultural records of solar eclipses show the lengths of days have changed." End quote. The page explains that models of eclipse shadows suggest days have lengthened slightly since twelve hundred B C, because otherwise the shadows would not have fallen where ancient scribes recorded them.

I should flag a limit. That page gives one headline figure, and I did not fetch the underlying research paper, so treat one point eight as an approximate number.

And finally, one thing that connects all three.

The tool we use to keep our clocks in step with Earth's slow rotation is the leap second. The International Bureau of Weights and Measures publishes a resolution passed in twenty twenty-two by the general conference on weights and measures. It decides that the maximum allowed difference between Earth-rotation time and atomic time will be increased in, or before, twenty thirty-five. The same page says a leap second is currently applied when that difference is predicted to approach point nine seconds.

It also says a draft resolution was due for the next general conference in twenty twenty-six. I could not confirm this morning what happened there, so I will leave it as unknown.

That's your digest. It's Saturday, October seventeenth. Have a good one.
