## Full script
Good morning. It's Sunday, October eighteenth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: book reviews.

Two new novels from this fall, both by writers with serious reputations. One looks centuries ahead. The other looks inward, at a single failing mind. And critics have been far from unanimous about either.

Our first book is Ply, by Hernan Diaz, published on September twenty-ninth, according to Kirkus Reviews' fall preview list.

Here is what it is about, without giving anything away. In a review in the Boston Globe, the critic Priscilla Gilman describes a novel set several centuries in the future, in a city that resembles New York. Democracy has collapsed. Private firms control security and electricity. Public health has deteriorated. The first half follows an orphaned young woman who survives on the fringes by stealing electricity and selling it on the black market. The second half shifts to a scientific project.

Now, what the critics said. The review aggregator Bookmarks lists fourteen reviews of Ply. By its tally, nine are favorable, two are mixed, and three are pans. So this is a divided reception.

On the favorable side, Gilman, in the Boston Globe on September twenty-second, called it, Quote: "A vision of the future both deeply disconcerting and oddly exhilarating." End quote. Kirkus Reviews called it, Quote: "A brainy, challenging, and engrossing trek into a battered but unbeaten future." End quote.

On the other side, Kathryn Schulz, in The New Yorker, wrote that, Quote: "the grand intellectual ambitions of Ply finally overwhelm the other desiderata of fiction." End quote. Dwight Garner, in the New York Times, was blunter, Quote: "it's suffocating, as if someone has thrown a tarp over your head." End quote. Both of those quotes are as listed by Bookmarks, which I read rather than the full reviews.

Even Gilman, who admired the book, had a reservation. She wrote that, Quote: "the novel's second half felt disappointing and the playing with pronouns and tenses a bit gimmicky." End quote.

Others weighed in as well. Jacob Brogan, in The Atlantic, wrote that it, Quote: "Fails to fulfill the promise of every great end-of-the-world novel." End quote. Barbara Conaty, in Library Journal, was on the opposite side, writing that it, Quote: "makes every page exhilarating and meaningful." End quote. And Sam Corbett, in The Times of London, was mixed, writing that, Quote: "Ply seems intent on telling multiple stories at once and can't fully commit to any of them." End quote.

Reading across all of this, the disagreement is not about whether Diaz is ambitious. Everyone agrees he is. It is about whether the ambition serves the story or swamps it. That is the useful thing to know before you pick it up.

So who is Ply for? Based on this coverage, it suits readers who love big ideas and ambitious structure, and who can forgive thin characters. The common complaints across reviews, per Bookmarks, were weak characterization, didactic dialogue, and a second half that some found less satisfying.

Our second book is Switzy, by Emma Cline, which Kirkus lists with a publication date of September eighth.

The novel follows a man named David Hastings, whose memory and sense of self are fading as he nears a planned assisted death. Reviewers say it deals with dementia and with the ethics of assisted dying. That is heavy material, and reviewers say so.

Bookmarks lists fourteen reviews here too. Eight are raves, three are positive, and three are mixed. That is a much warmer reception than Ply got.

Cory Oldweiler, in the Boston Globe on September first, called it, Quote: "A bold and arresting departure from Cline's earlier work." End quote. Kirkus described it as, Quote: "Unexpected, beautifully controlled, and almost dangerously captivating." End quote. Hillary Kelly, in The Atlantic, used just one phrase: Quote: "Gut-twisting." End quote.

The cooler voices were gentler than the Ply pans. Alexandra Jacobs, in the New York Times on September sixth, asked, Quote: "So why am I not completely head over heels for Switzy?" End quote. And Abhrajyoti Chakraborty, in the New Statesman, wrote that, Quote: "Cline has a habit of piling on too many metaphors about death." End quote.

More voices. Chris Power, in The Guardian, wrote that the book, Quote: "Will probably stay with me for ever." End quote. Emma Alpern, in Vulture, wrote, Quote: "This book doesn't try to diagnose our collective ills. What's left is something smaller, more tense and tender." End quote. And Nick Roberts, in the Los Angeles Review of Books, who was mixed overall, still granted that, Quote: "Cline nimbly imagines a mind in the throes of unraveling." End quote.

According to Bookmarks, critics praised the prose, the emotional tension, and the portrait of a fracturing mind. The common criticisms were sentimentality, an overused metaphor style, a weaker secondary storyline, and doubts about whether David is likable and whether the medical details are plausible.

Who would enjoy it? Readers who like close, intimate fiction and can sit with a hard subject. If you want something more cheerful, this is probably not the one.

And finally, one thing worth trying. If a book divides critics this sharply, read the opening pages of each side's favorite and decide for yourself. With Ply and Switzy, a few pages will tell you quickly whether the style is for you.

That's your digest. It's Sunday, October eighteenth. Have a good one.
