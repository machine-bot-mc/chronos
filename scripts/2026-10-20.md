## Full script
Good morning. It's Tuesday, October twentieth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: business.

Three Canadian energy deals from the last six weeks. A merger that creates a seventy-two billion dollar utility, a foreign buyer for offshore Newfoundland oil, and a ten billion dollar tie-up in the Clearwater. The common thread is a bet on long-lived energy assets.

Story one. Emera swallows Canadian Utilities.

On October sixth, BNN Bloomberg reported that Halifax-based Emera will acquire Canadian Utilities in an all-stock deal valued at fourteen point three billion dollars. The combined enterprise value is about seventy-two billion dollars.

Here are the mechanics. Canadian Utilities Class A shareholders, other than ATCO, get zero point seven five five Emera shares for each share they hold. Class B holders get zero point eight one nine. That values a Class A share at about fifty-one dollars and fifty-seven cents, a premium of roughly zero point seven percent to Monday's close, according to LSEG data.

ATCO is the parent. It holds nearly thirty-seven percent of the non-voting shares and all the voting shares. As part of the deal, ATCO will separate into its own publicly traded industrial services company.

The combined company keeps the Emera name and its headquarters in Halifax. It would have about six million customers and twelve regulated utilities. Florida would be its largest jurisdiction at about forty-five percent, and Alberta second at about thirty-four percent.

Emera says the deal strengthens its finances as power companies invest to meet rising demand. It supports a thirty-two billion dollar capital program through twenty thirty. The deal is expected to close in the third or fourth quarter of twenty twenty-seven.

The caveats. The premium is tiny. In morning trading, Emera fell one point two percent, Canadian Utilities rose one point four percent, and ATCO jumped twelve point eight percent. The article does not say what approvals are required, so I can't tell you that.

Why it matters. Emera's argument is that meeting rising power demand takes a bigger balance sheet. For Canadians, the notable point is that the larger of its two main jurisdictions would be in Florida, not Canada.

Story two. A UK producer buys into Newfoundland.

On October fifth, Ithaca Energy, a North Sea producer, announced it will buy Suncor's offshore Canada assets. Ithaca's own release calls it a transformational strategic position and its first move into Canada.

The price is eight hundred sixty million U.S. dollars in cash. There is also up to two hundred fifty million more, tied to Brent oil prices staying above set thresholds through September twenty twenty-eight.

The assets are a forty-eight percent operated stake in Terra Nova, plus non-operated stakes in the White Rose fields, which Cenovus operates. Over the twelve months to June thirtieth, net production was about sixteen and a half thousand barrels of oil equivalent a day across the two. Ithaca says the assets earned about two hundred thirty-five million U.S. dollars in adjusted EBITDAX over that period.

Ithaca expects the deal to close in the first half of twenty twenty-seven. It needs Competition Act approval in Canada and other government approvals. If it doesn't close by the end of twenty twenty-seven, either side can walk away.

Limits. The reserve and resource figures are management estimates, not independently verified. And the release itself gives inconsistent numbers for remaining resources, so I'll leave those out.

Why it matters. Ithaca describes these as long-life, low-decline assets, and is paying real money to enter Canada's East Coast. Suncor is the seller, and what it plans to do with the proceeds isn't in the release.

Story three. The Clearwater gets a champion.

On September eighth, Tamarack Valley Energy and Headwater Exploration announced an all-stock combination, valued in their release at ten billion dollars. The release does not state the currency or whether that is enterprise value, so treat the headline number with care.

Each Headwater share converts into one Tamarack share. Tamarack issues about two hundred thirty-eight million shares. Tamarack holders end up with about sixty-six and a half percent and Headwater holders about thirty-three and a half percent.

The companies say the result is the largest Clearwater producer, with run-rate production above eighty thousand barrels of oil equivalent a day. They project over fifty million dollars a year in synergies, and a twenty percent dividend increase, from five cents to six cents a quarter, if the deal closes.

Some non-core assets, including heavy oil prospects in Saskatchewan and a gas asset in New Brunswick, are spun into a new company called Tributary Exploration.

Closing is targeted for about the middle of the fourth quarter of this year. Shareholder meetings are expected in November. Court, Competition Act and TSX approvals are also required.

The company warns that its projections carry risks, including commodity prices, tariffs and pipeline outages. The synergies and growth figures are the companies' own estimates.

Why it matters. Scale is the stated strategy. The companies say pooling would make the only publicly traded pure-play Clearwater producer, with a free funds flow per share lift of more than ten percent. Whether that happens is their projection, not a result.

And finally, one thing worth trying. Every one of these deals comes with a company release. Read the closing conditions section. It tells you more about the real risk than the headline price does.

That's your digest. It's Tuesday, October twentieth. Have a good one.
