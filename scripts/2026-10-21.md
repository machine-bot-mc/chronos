## Full script
Good morning. It's Wednesday, October twenty-first, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: health.

Three stories. A large American study on ultra-processed food and bowel cancer. A lung cancer trial where an expensive immunotherapy did not add benefit. And Canada's new cancer statistics. Remember, this is reporting on research, not medical advice.

Story one. Ultra-processed food and bowel cancer.

Ultra-processed food gets blamed for a lot. A new analysis suggests the picture for bowel cancer is more mixed than the headlines.

The study was published in PLOS Medicine on October fifteenth. It was reported by ecancer on October sixteenth. The lead author is Erikka Loftfield of the American National Cancer Institute, and the work was funded by that institute's own research program.

It used the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study. That is a cohort of four hundred sixty-one thousand, six hundred eighty-two American adults, aged fifty to seventy-one, enrolled in nineteen ninety-five and ninety-six. They filled in one food questionnaire at the start. Researchers then followed them until twenty eighteen, a median of eighteen point eight years.

The main finding: total ultra-processed food intake was not associated with a higher risk of colorectal cancer.

But the details differ by food type. Whole grains, fibre, dairy and calcium that came from ultra-processed foods were linked to lower risk. Meat from ultra-processed foods was linked to higher risk. And swapping ten percent of ultra-processed food, by weight, for minimally processed food was associated with two percent lower risk.

Now the limits. This is an observational study, so it shows associations, not cause. Diet was measured only once, in the mid nineteen nineties, long before today's food supply. And the group was mostly older and white, so the results may not apply to younger or more diverse people.

Why it matters. It does not say ultra-processed food is harmless. It says the label is too blunt. What the food is made of seems to matter, and processed meat is the clearest signal here.

Story two. A lung cancer trial that came up empty.

This one is about a treatment that did not add benefit. That is useful to know too.

The JAMA journal published the ALCHEMIST EA fifty-one forty-two trial on June first, twenty twenty-six. It was covered by the ECOG-ACRIN research group. The question: after surgery for non-small cell lung cancer, and after standard chemotherapy or radiation, does a year of the immunotherapy nivolumab reduce the chance the cancer comes back?

The trial was randomized. Nine hundred thirty-five people were assigned, by chance, to nivolumab or to observation. Four hundred sixty-six got the drug. Four hundred sixty-nine were simply watched. The trial was run at three hundred seventy-eight centres in the United States.

The result: no significant improvement. Median disease-free survival was seventy-one point three months with nivolumab and sixty-eight point eight months with observation. Overall survival was similar. Even in patients whose tumours had high levels of the marker PD-L1, the benefit was not significant. Median follow-up was seventy-two point six months, about six years.

There was a cost. Twenty-five percent of patients on nivolumab had serious side effects, grade three to five, including two deaths.

The caveats. The trial was open-label, so patients and doctors knew who got what. Enrolment timing after surgery varied. And eighty-nine percent of participants were white, so we know less about other groups.

Why it matters. Immunotherapy has helped in many cancers, but this shows it does not help in every setting. Testing it properly spares patients side effects with no proven gain. One note: the JAMA page says an AI model helped draft its summary text, so I have stuck to the numbers in it.

Story three. Canada's cancer numbers for twenty twenty-six.

This is a Canadian report, so it is closer to home. It was published in the Canadian Medical Association Journal on April thirteenth, twenty twenty-six, and reported by CTV News. It comes from the Canadian Cancer Society, Statistics Canada and the Public Health Agency of Canada.

It estimates more than two hundred fifty thousand new cancer cases this year, and nearly ninety thousand cancer deaths. About forty-two percent of people in Canada are expected to be diagnosed with cancer in their lifetime.

The good news: rates of diagnosis and death are falling for about two-thirds of cancer types, including colorectal, bladder, lung and leukemia.

The worrying news: cervical, head and neck, pancreatic, uterine and melanoma cancers are rising. Pancreatic cancer is estimated at seven thousand five hundred cases, up from seven thousand one hundred in the last report. Epidemiologist Jennifer Gillis, a co-author, linked the cervical and head and neck increases to HPV, which she said is almost entirely preventable through vaccination.

Lung cancer is still the biggest killer, with about thirty-two thousand nine hundred new cases. It causes one in five cancer deaths. Gillis credited tobacco control for part of the decline.

A caveat. These are estimates, projections from registry data, not a head count. And the report looked at the population as a whole, not by age group. CTV notes that rising bowel cancer in adults under fifty comes from other research, not this report.

Why it matters. It shows prevention working, through tobacco control, and where it is not yet, like HPV vaccination.

And finally, one thing worth trying. Next time you read a headline about a food or a drug, look for three things: how many people, whether it was a trial or just observation, and who paid for it. All three were in today's stories.

That's your digest. It's Wednesday, October twenty-first. Have a good one.
