## Full script
Good morning. It's Thursday, October twenty-second, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: personal AI.

Three stories from the past week. Gemini starts reading your email and photos in the car. Notion's agents can now work on a single task for more than a day. And ChatGPT adds a PIN lock for private conversations.

Story one. Gemini in the car gets personal.

Google announced on October twentieth that Gemini in cars can now use a feature called Personal Intelligence. That's according to Business Today, which reported it on October twenty-first. With your permission, Gemini can draw on Gmail, Google Calendar, Google Photos, Google Maps, YouTube and Search to answer requests.

The examples in the report are practical. Finding a doctor's appointment even though you never saved an address. Locating a car wash that appears in one of your photos. Or suggesting a vegetarian-friendly indoor restaurant along your route.

Now the limits. It's opt in. You turn on Personal Intelligence and choose which apps Gemini may use, and you can switch it off in Gemini settings. It's for personal Google accounts, for people eighteen and older. Work, business and school accounts are excluded. The report says it's rolling out on Android Auto globally, with cars that have Google built in expected to follow in the coming months. Those cars only give personalized answers while your phone is connected. The excluded regions are the European Economic Area, Switzerland, the United Kingdom and Nigeria. Canada isn't on that list, but the report doesn't confirm a Canadian date, so check your own phone. It also doesn't mention languages or pricing, so those are unknown.

Why it matters. The assistant is moving from answering general questions to knowing your life. For anyone building these tools, the lesson is that permission design, meaning what the assistant can see and when, is now part of the product.

Story two. Notion's agents get longer legs.

On October nineteenth, Notion co-founder Akshay Kothari published a post called Agents, rebuilt to do more for less. Quote: "Notion Agents can now work on a single task for more than twenty-four hours, up from five minutes." End quote. Notion says each session runs on a dedicated service, so work continues through restarts.

The post also describes a router called Auto, which sends each task to the model that best balances quality, speed and cost. If a model isn't making progress, Auto switches to another. Notion's claim: across more than twenty common use cases, Auto gave similar results to Opus five point five and Astra at about ninety percent lower average cost. That is Notion's own evaluation. It hasn't been independently verified.

On pricing, Business and Enterprise seats get fifteen hundred Standard credits and five hundred Select credits a month. With Standard, Notion picks the model. With Select, you pick it, or you run Custom Agents. The new system is live for new customers now. Existing customers move over on November second. Notion also says a bring your own key option is in alpha for Enterprise only.

Why it matters. Long-running agents change the job from asking a question to handing off a project. The catch is cost and trust. A task that runs for a day can burn credits and go wrong quietly, so the credit system is as much a part of this story as the twenty-four hours.

Story three. ChatGPT adds locked chats.

According to OpenAI's ChatGPT release notes, as mirrored by Releasebot, on October twentieth ChatGPT began rolling out locked chats. Quote: "Locked chats let you keep selected conversations in your account while hiding them from regular chat history." End quote.

A locked chat disappears from history, pinned chats and search. You open it with a four-digit account PIN or your device's own authentication. To lock a chat, open its menu and choose Lock chat. It's rolling out gradually to Free, Go, Plus and Pro users, on web, iPhone, Android and the new Mac and Windows desktop apps.

The limit worth knowing: this hides conversations. It doesn't change retention or model-improvement settings, which stay as they were. So it's privacy from someone glancing at your screen, not a change in what OpenAI keeps.

Why it matters. Shared tablets and family laptops are where this helps. As people put health questions, money worries and gift ideas into one assistant, hiding a conversation from the next person who picks up the device becomes a basic need.

And finally, one thing worth trying. If you build for non-technical founders, look at the permission screens in these three products. Each lets the user choose what the assistant sees, and each says plainly what it doesn't change. That's a pattern worth copying.

That's your digest. It's Thursday, October twenty-second. Have a good one.
