Good morning. It's Friday, October twenty-third, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: international news.

Three stories. Russia agrees in principle to restart three-way peace talks, even as missiles hit Kyiv. Iran reports seven oil tankers damaged in the Strait of Hormuz. And a software flaw found with the help of AI sends governments scrambling to patch websites.

Story one. Talks on, strikes continue.

The Associated Press reported on Wednesday, October twenty-first, that Russian President Vladimir Putin has agreed in principle to resume three-way talks with the United States and Ukraine. His foreign affairs adviser, Yuri Ushakov, said they would restart after November tenth. Ushakov said the talks would focus on, quote: "building a security system for Russia and Ukraine after the conflict ends." End quote.

The announcement followed a meeting of about three hours in Saint Petersburg between Putin and Steve Witkoff, an envoy of President Donald Trump. Russia also freed Stephen Hubbard, a seventy-four-year-old retired American teacher who had been imprisoned on mercenary charges. Witkoff called it a goodwill gesture. According to the AP, earlier rounds this year, in Abu Dhabi and Geneva, produced little beyond prisoner exchanges.

The fighting did not pause. In a second AP report, dated Thursday, October twenty-second, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Kyiv was hit by twenty-one ballistic missiles in three waves. He said at least four people were killed in Ukraine, with dozens wounded, including five children. Russia's Defense Ministry said its air defenses shot down eight hundred and eight Ukrainian drones overnight. Moscow's mayor said six hundred and thirty were aimed at the capital. The AP notes these figures come from the parties themselves and were not independently verified.

The two sides read the moment differently. Zelenskyy said Russia, quote: "has not changed the way it wages this war." End quote. Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov said Washington is, quote: "doing a lot" to help a peaceful settlement, and accused European capitals of provoking escalation. End quote. Witkoff also drew criticism in Ukraine and the United States for calling Putin a humanitarian.

Why it matters. A date after November tenth is a plan, not a ceasefire. No date is set, and the strikes show neither side is waiting for one.

Story two. Tankers in the Strait of Hormuz.

Also from the AP, on Thursday, October twenty-second. Iranian state television reported that seven oil tankers were hit overnight, either by attacks or by naval mines. Citing a Revolutionary Guard navy statement, it said four tankers were transferring oil when they were targeted and caught fire. Three others were immobilized after hitting mines.

Here is what is not known. The report gave no casualty figures and no flags for the ships. The AP does not say who carried out the attacks, and the claim had not been independently verified. If it holds up, the AP says, it could be the largest number of vessels damaged in the waterway in a single day since the Iran war began on February twenty-eighth.

There is a workaround, and it is telling. Lloyd's List Intelligence said some operators move oil through the strait at night, along a lane protected by the American military near Oman's coast, with tracking systems and phones switched off. The oil is then transferred to tankers in the Gulf of Oman bound for Asia.

Saudi Arabia is also routing around the strait. Aramco chief executive Amin Nasser said the East-West pipeline's capacity has risen from two million to seven million barrels a day.

Meanwhile, the Houthis claimed responsibility for an attack on King Fahd International Airport in eastern Saudi Arabia. Saudi aviation authorities said two people died, a Saudi citizen and an Indian national, and eight were injured.

Why it matters. Much of the world's oil moves through this waterway. Even partly confirmed attacks raise the cost and risk of shipping it, and that reaches fuel prices far from the Gulf.

Story three. An AI-assisted security scare.

The AP reported on Thursday, October twenty-second, on flaws in PHP, a programming language the AP says powers about seventy percent of websites worldwide. The FBI's cyber division warned on Wednesday that attackers could exploit the flaws to run code remotely. It advised operators who cannot update right away to take their sites offline.

OpenAI said in a blog post that it led a coalition of researchers and security providers that worked on the flaws for two months. It called them, quote: "one of the most impactful security issues in recent memory." End quote. It said AI appeared to find them before attackers did. The PHP Foundation said late Wednesday that fixes are released.

Cloudflare's chief executive, Matthew Prince, said there were millions of exploitation attempts in the hours after the announcement. Japan's prime minister, Sanae Takaichi, urged administrators to check whether they use PHP and patch. Some Japanese government sites were taken offline as a precaution, and Japan reported no damage. The United Kingdom said limited government services might be briefly offline, but no large critical services were affected.

What the report does not say. It gives no technical identifiers or version numbers, does not name the AI system, and does not confirm that any attack succeeded.

Why it matters. For anyone who builds or hosts websites, the lesson is simple. Check whether your stack runs PHP, and apply the update.

And finally, one thing worth trying. If you host a site for a client, take ten minutes today to ask your hosting provider whether the PHP update has been applied.
