## Full script
Good morning. It's Sunday, October twenty-fifth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: book reviews.

Two novels from this year's Booker Prize shortlist. One is by a past winner returning to the Scottish islands. The other is by a Pulitzer winner, writing about a long marriage and a troubled country. Critics have mostly loved both, with a few real reservations.

The Booker Prize shortlist was reported by the Irish Examiner on September twenty-third. Six novels made it. The winner will be announced on November ninth, at a ceremony in London. So neither of these has won yet.

Our first book is John of John, by Douglas Stuart.

Stuart won the Booker in twenty twenty for Shuggie Bain. This is his third novel. According to the review aggregator Bookmarks, which lists excerpts from critics, it is set on a windswept croft in a Hebridean island community. It follows a repressed, religious father named John, and his son, Cal. The story is about secrets, about things everyone knows but nobody says, and about what it costs to live a lie. The Irish Examiner describes the son as a young man who returns to Harris, a Scottish island.

Now, what the critics said. Bookmarks lists seventeen reviews. By its count, thirteen are raves, three are positive, and one is mixed.

Hamilton Cain, in the Boston Globe, called it, Quote: "A muscular narrative with scrupulous technique. It's his finest work yet." End quote. Mark Harris, in the New York Times Book Review, said it is, Quote: "Moving, suspenseful, completely-worth-your-time." End quote. Helen Cullen, in the Irish Times, wrote, Quote: "A masterly storyteller with a poet's prose, this is Stuart's best yet." End quote.

The Guardian's reviewer, Yagnishsing Dawoor, was positive, and called it, Quote: "Intimate yet epic in scale." End quote.

The one mixed review came from Sam Sacks in the Wall Street Journal. His complaint was specific. Quote: "The reasons for John's ferocious emotional repression are not fully explored." End quote. Bookmarks also notes that some critics found the final stretch melodramatic, and some found the characters frustrating.

Who would enjoy it? Readers who like quiet, landscape-heavy family drama, and who liked Stuart's earlier books.

Our second book is The Things We Never Say, by Elizabeth Strout.

Strout is a Pulitzer Prize winner. The Irish Examiner says this is her eleventh novel. She was shortlisted for the Booker in twenty twenty-two for Oh William, and nominated in twenty sixteen for My Name Is Lucy Barton. According to Bookmarks, this new book is not part of her Olive Kitteridge or Lucy Barton series. It follows a long marriage tested by secrets and things held back, with a protagonist named Artie. It deals with loneliness, social class, and the current political moment.

Bookmarks lists nineteen reviews. Ten are raves, five are positive, four are mixed, and none are pans.

Adam Begley, in the Atlantic, wrote, Quote: "She's not a minimalist, but Elizabeth Strout does more with less than any writer I can think of." End quote. Julia Klein, in the Los Angeles Times, said, Quote: "Here is Strout at her most emotionally precise." End quote. Diana Evans, in the Financial Times, called it, Quote: "A profound, resplendent novel that stares our present moment in the face." End quote.

The Times of London's Johanna Thomas-Corr went further. Quote: "The first great work of fiction about Trump's presidency." End quote.

Maureen Corrigan, on NPR, was positive. She noted that, Quote: "Strout's signature subjects of loneliness and class humiliation reappear." End quote.

Now the mixed side. Priscilla Gilman, in the Boston Globe, wrote, Quote: "this is by far her bleakest book." End quote. Rohan Maitzen, in the Times Literary Supplement, called it, Quote: "Uncharacteristically dispiriting." End quote. Ellen Peirson-Hagger, in the New Statesman, said, Quote: "its philosophical conclusion is too neat, too easy." End quote.

So the pattern is clear. Admirers praise her precision and empathy. The doubters find this one darker, and its ending too tidy.

Who would enjoy it? Readers who want a close, character-driven novel with a political edge, and who can handle a bleak mood.

One caveat. I only had the short excerpts that Bookmarks lists, not the full reviews. And I could not confirm the publication dates of either book from the pages I read.

And finally, one thing worth trying. If you read only one, notice how each critic's complaint matches the other's praise. Both books are quiet, and both are about what people leave unsaid.

That's your digest. It's Sunday, October twenty-fifth. Have a good one.
