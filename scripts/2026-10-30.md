## Full script
Good morning. It's Friday, October thirtieth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: international news.

Three stories. Israel's election leaves Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc without a majority. Russia and Ukraine hold a rare local truce so aid can reach the occupied city of Oleshky. And Canada and the European Union launch a new partnership, with Canada aiming to become the bloc's first associate member.

Story one. Israel's deadlock.

The Associated Press reported on Friday, October thirtieth, that Netanyahu's bloc won fifty-three seats in the hundred and twenty seat Knesset. A government needs at least sixty-one. The main opposition bloc, led by former military chief Gadi Eisenkot, won forty-eight. Arab-led parties won nineteen seats, which the AP describes as a historic showing.

Here is what happens next, according to the AP. The results must be certified and sent to President Isaac Herzog next Wednesday. He will then ask someone to try to form a government. That could be Netanyahu, as leader of the largest party, or another figure the party leaders back. Netanyahu stays in charge in the meantime. The new Knesset is expected to be sworn in on November tenth. If repeated attempts fail, Israel would head to a new election early next year.

There is a dispute. Netanyahu's party, Likud, has claimed the election was stolen, possibly with foreign help. Israeli media reported that the party accused Qatar of involvement, and Qatar called that, quote: "ridiculous and dangerous." End quote. The election committee said, quote: "allegations of systematic violations of electoral integrity are baseless." End quote. Its chair said he could not verify the interference claims and asked the Shin Bet security service to investigate. The AP says the allegations are unsubstantiated.

Why it matters. Any path to a government is hard. Netanyahu has warned against a coalition with Arab parties, and the AP notes the opposition has never allied with them for long. The AP also notes that if Netanyahu leaves office, an official inquiry into the October seventh attack would almost certainly follow.

Story two. A truce for Oleshky.

The AP reported on Friday that a humanitarian mission entered Oleshky, a Russian-occupied city in Ukraine's Kherson region. The AP calls it the most significant truce of the four-and-a-half-year war. A local ceasefire has held for four days and was due to last through Sunday evening.

Russia and Ukraine issued identical statements. Quote: "Everyone who wishes to leave Oleshky will have the opportunity to do so safely." End quote. The convoy included twenty-five buses, plus ambulances carrying food and medicine. Authorities and volunteers say about two thousand people remain in the city. Two people were killed while demining the routes.

The White House says the deal was brokered by first lady Melania Trump and that the United States played a central role. That is the White House's claim, as the AP reports it. A volunteer who used to live in Oleshky says many Russian troops are in the city and that residents are being pressured to evacuate to Russia rather than Ukraine. Those are her claims, and the AP did not confirm them. The evacuation route was not disclosed.

The truce is local. Russia said its air defenses downed more than a thousand Ukrainian drones overnight, which is Russia's claim. Ukraine's air force said Russia launched missiles and scores of drones.

Why it matters. It shows that narrow, practical deals are still possible even without a wider ceasefire. Whether it holds past Sunday is not yet known.

Story three. Canada and Europe.

The AP reported on Friday that Prime Minister Mark Carney and European leaders ended a two-day summit in Montreal. Carney, European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and European Council President António Costa launched what they call the Alliance for the Future.

Carney said he expects to finish negotiations in June on a partnership that would make Canada the bloc's first associate member. He described it as, quote: "the deepest possible partnership, short of accession." End quote. Von der Leyen said, quote: "We know that Canada cannot be a full-fledged member of the European Union." End quote. The joint declaration did not mention associate membership. It promised an, quote: "ever-closer association," End quote, by the twenty twenty-seven summit. The AP does not define the status formally, and says some European governments resist it.

There were concrete steps. Talks concluded on a digital trade agreement and a competition agreement. Canada will join a European supercomputing initiative. The two sides will study exporting Canadian liquefied natural gas to Europe, and will explore linking instant payment systems.

Why it matters. The United States took seventy-one point seven percent of Canada's merchandise exports in twenty twenty-five, the AP says. President Donald Trump suggested the arrangement could be a, quote: "hostile act." End quote. He also threatened tariffs on Europe. So this is a long-term bet on diversifying trade, with real pushback attached.

And finally, one thing worth watching: November tenth, when Israel's new Knesset is expected to be sworn in.

That's your digest. It's Friday, October thirtieth. Have a good one.
