## Full script
Good morning. It's Saturday, October thirty-first, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: fascinating facts, with one theme. The octopus.

Four facts coming up. Hearts and blue blood. An animal that thinks with its arms. Two kinds of sleep. And a way of adapting to cold that skips evolution altogether.

Today's theme: the octopus.

Fact one. Three hearts, and blue blood.

Smithsonian Magazine, in a piece by Rachel Nuwer, originally published on October thirty-first, twenty thirteen, and updated in March of twenty twenty-four, states it plainly. Octopuses have three hearts. Two of them move blood past the gills, where it drops off carbon dioxide and picks up oxygen. The third delivers that oxygen-rich blood to the organs and muscles.

There is a catch. Smithsonian reports that the third heart actually stops beating when the octopus swims. That is one reason octopuses tend to crawl rather than swim. Swimming, for them, is tiring.

The blood is blue too. Human blood carries oxygen with an iron-based protein called hemoglobin. Octopus blood uses a copper-containing protein called hemocyanin. Smithsonian says the copper is what makes the blood appear blue. The same article notes that this blood is sensitive to acidity, so falling ocean pH can make it harder for the animals to get enough oxygen.

Why it matters: it is a reminder that evolution solves the same problem, moving oxygen around a body, in completely different ways.

Fact two. An animal that tastes and thinks with its arms.

The same Smithsonian article says that two thirds of an octopus's neurons are in its arms, not its head. Some arms can crack open shellfish while the rest of the animal is doing something else. Severed arms have even reacted when pinched in experiments.

The arms also taste. Smithsonian reported on a study published in the journal Cell in early November of twenty twenty. Researchers led by Lena van Giesen, an evolutionary biologist at Harvard University, studied the California two-spot octopus. They found that the suckers have two types of sensory cells. One responds to touch, much like cells in other animals. The other is new to science. It has receptor proteins that respond to chemicals, such as fish extract.

Each receptor protein is tuned to a different chemical in the environment. The experiments showed that the suckers can taste at least one class of poorly water-soluble compounds, called terpenoids.

Now the limits. Only one species was studied, and only that one class of compounds was tested directly. Scientists still don't know the full range of tastes these cells detect.

Fact three. Two kinds of sleep.

Science News reported on March twenty-fifth, twenty twenty-one, on a study published that day in the journal iScience. Researchers at the Brain Institute of the Federal University of Rio Grande do Norte in Brazil, led by Sylvia Medeiros, watched four wild octopuses of the species Octopus insularis.

They saw two sleep states. In quiet sleep, the octopus is pale and motionless, with its pupils narrowed to slits. Then, about every half hour, comes active sleep. The eyes move rapidly, the suckers contract, the muscles twitch, and bright colors move across the body. Science News says it is over after about forty seconds. The octopuses spent less than one percent of their days in active sleep.

Is the octopus dreaming? Nobody knows. Sidarta Ribeiro, a coauthor, is quoted saying that no one knows what the octopuses experience during these sessions. He says more work is needed before anyone can say they dream.

Fact four. Editing the message instead of the gene.

On June eighth, twenty twenty-three, a study in Cell reported how octopuses cope with cold. The University of Chicago described it on June twenty-second. Again the animal was the California two-spot octopus. Researchers kept some in warm tanks at about twenty-two degrees Celsius, and some in cool tanks at about thirteen degrees.

Cells copy genes into RNA, which is then used to build proteins. Octopuses can edit that RNA, changing the proteins without changing the genome. In the cold, the team saw protein-altering editing increase at more than thirteen thousand sites in the animals' nervous systems. Cold-acclimated animals showed significant increases at thirteen thousand two hundred and eighty-five sites. Warm-tank animals showed increases at five hundred and fifty.

In two example proteins, the editing changed how they worked. One moves cargo inside nerve cells, and the editing changed its transport speed.

The caveats are real. The editing is temporary. Only two proteins were examined in detail. The follow-up experiments suggested it helps with gradual temperature shifts, but not rapid ones. And the idea that this is tied to cephalopod intelligence is, as the university presents it, a hypothesis.

And finally, one thing worth trying. Next time you see an octopus in a documentary, watch the arms. Each one is behaving as if it has opinions of its own, and now you know why.

That's your digest. It's Saturday, October thirty-first. Have a good one.
