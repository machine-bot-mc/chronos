Good morning. It's Tuesday, November third, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: business.

We have three stories. The Bank of Canada raises its rate. Washington keeps reshuffling its tariff list on Canadian goods. And Meta posts big revenue growth alongside a very large legal bill.

Story one. The Bank of Canada raises rates.

On October twenty-eighth, the Bank of Canada raised its overnight rate target by a quarter of a percentage point, to two and a half percent. The Bank's own announcement also puts the Bank Rate at two and three quarter percent, and the deposit rate at two point four five percent.

The reasoning is about inflation. The Bank says inflation has been around three percent since April. It expects about three and a half percent by the end of the year. Excluding gasoline, inflation was two point two percent in September, and the Bank says its core measures remain close to two percent. So this is mostly an energy story. The Bank points to persistently high oil prices, and says the conflict in the Middle East is now in its eighth month with no clear path to resolution.

The Bank's worry is spillover. Businesses have so far absorbed many higher costs. The Bank expects more of that to be passed on to customers. It wants to stop energy-driven price rises from spreading into other goods and services.

On growth, the economy grew three point three percent in the second quarter. The Bank sees about two percent in the third. For the full year it projects one percent growth, and two percent in both twenty twenty-seven and twenty twenty-eight. It expects growth to slow in the fourth quarter because of new United States tariffs. Unemployment has sat around six and a half percent for several months.

The Bank says it may need to raise the rate further, but will go carefully. The next announcement is December ninth.

Here's why it matters. If you carry variable-rate debt, or you're pricing a product for Canadian customers, borrowing costs are heading up while demand is expected to soften. The Bank is also saying it does not yet know how the trade fight will play out.

Story two. The tariff list keeps changing.

Staying with trade. According to a client alert from the law firm Dorsey and Whitney, dated August twenty-seventh, the United States began charging fifty percent tariffs on a range of Canadian goods on August twenty-second. The legal basis is Section three thirty-eight of the Tariff Act of nineteen thirty. The stated grounds were trade tensions over alcohol, autos and dairy. Canada responded with counter-tariffs of fifteen to fifty percent on American goods, starting September eighth.

The part that is new is the churn. The logistics company Flexport reported on October twenty-seventh that the list changes again on October thirtieth. That is the third change in three months. Plywood, plastic wrap, cardboard boxes and food-related machinery come off the list. Cocoa powder, paints, medium-density fiberboard, carpets and rugs, air conditioners and canoes go on. Flexport only gives one rate, fifty percent for plywood through October twenty-ninth. It does not state rates for the other items.

Flexport also notes that on October twentieth, an importer filed the first legal challenge to these tariffs at the United States Court of International Trade. The outcome and timing are unknown.

The big picture comes from the Bank of Canada's report. It estimates the average United States tariff rate on Canadian goods at seven and a half percent, up from five percent in July. Canada's average rate on American goods rose from one and a half percent to three point nine percent. The Bank also says about eighty percent of Canadian goods exports to the United States remain tariff-free. Energy, potash and critical minerals remain exempt from the new United States tariffs.

Here's why it matters. For most exporters, the tariffs hit a narrow slice of trade. But a list that changes every month makes it hard to plan inventory or price contracts. If you ship physical goods across the border, check the current list rather than last month's.

Story three. Meta: growth, and a nine point nine billion dollar charge.

On October twenty-first, Meta reported third quarter results. Revenue was sixty-four point six billion dollars, up twenty-six percent from a year earlier. Daily active people across its apps averaged three point six four billion in September.

But operating income fell thirty-seven percent, to about thirteen billion dollars. The operating margin dropped to twenty percent, from forty percent a year before. The main reason is a nine point eight nine billion dollar legal charge. Meta says it relates to an August agreement with fifty-two state attorneys general over teen mental health and social media litigation. The agreement provides for payments of up to about eighteen point five nine billion dollars. Without the charge, Meta says operating income would have been about twenty-two point nine billion dollars.

On spending, Meta narrowed its full-year capital spending forecast to one hundred thirty-five to one hundred forty-five billion dollars. Third quarter capital spending alone was about thirty-five billion. It now expects total expenses of one hundred seventy-five to one hundred seventy-nine billion dollars for the year, raised to include the legal charge. For the fourth quarter it guides to revenue of seventy-three to seventy-six billion dollars.
