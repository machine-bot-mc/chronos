## Full script
Good morning. It's Wednesday, November fourth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: health.

Three studies, all announced today. A kudzu extract for binge drinking, a Japanese look at daily steps and healthcare costs, and a large American picture of substance related emergency care.

Story one. Kudzu and binge drinking.

Researchers at the University of California, San Francisco published a randomized trial in the American Journal of Psychiatry today, November fourth. It was reported in a university release on EurekAlert.

The trial enrolled one hundred twenty adults with alcohol use disorder who binge drank at least once a week. Half took a standardized kudzu extract. Half took a placebo. For twelve weeks, they took it about two and a half hours before they expected to drink heavily. Everyone also got brief weekly counseling.

Binge drinking days per week fell from about two point one five to one point four two in the kudzu group. In the placebo group, they went from two point one five to two point zero six. Weeks with any binge drinking fell from eighty-eight percent to fifty-eight percent with kudzu, and to eighty-one percent with placebo. Three months after treatment ended, the kudzu group still had fewer binge drinking days.

The limits matter. The release gives no effect sizes or statistical values. The study used a standardized formulation, and over the counter kudzu products can vary. The authors say larger studies are needed. The National Institutes of Health funded it, and the authors report no commercial financial ties.

Why it matters. This is a placebo controlled trial in people, so it can speak to cause, but it is one trial of one hundred twenty. Lead author Glenn-Milo Santos said, quote: "Binge drinking is incredibly common, but we don't have many tools to offer people who want to cut back." End quote. This is not a recommendation to buy a supplement.

Story two. Steps and healthcare costs in Osaka.

Osaka Metropolitan University researchers published this study in SSM Population Health on August third. The press release came out today. 

They linked step counts from a prefectural health app called A-Smile to health insurance claims. The data covered twenty thousand nine hundred fifty app users aged forty and over, from twenty twenty to twenty twenty-three.

For adults sixty-five and older, predicted healthcare costs were highest at low step counts and fell steeply as steps rose. The decline levelled off at about ten thousand six hundred steps a day. For adults forty to sixty-four, predicted costs were nearly flat across step counts. Author Haruka Kato said, quote: "predicted costs are far more sensitive to daily steps in this group." End quote.

The caveats. This is observational. The release says it shows associations, not causal effects. It reports no yen amounts or percentage savings. These were also long term app users, who may differ from the general public. That last point is my note, not the release's. It did not give a limitations section.

Why it matters. It adds to evidence that more movement tracks with lower costs in older adults. It does not prove that walking more would save money for any one person.

Story three. Alcohol and cannabis in emergency care.

Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis published this in the American Journal of Psychiatry today. It is observational, and it is American data.

The team used de-identified commercial insurance and Medicaid records. They looked at more than one point three million people aged sixteen to sixty-four with at least one substance related emergency encounter, between twenty sixteen and twenty twenty-three. That is over five million encounters.

Among teenagers aged sixteen to eighteen, eighty-four percent had at least one cannabis related encounter. Among those fifty-five to sixty-four, nineteen percent did. Alcohol went the other way. Seventy percent of the oldest group had an alcohol related encounter, against thirty-three percent of teens. Alcohol as the main reason for hospitalization rose fastest in ages twenty-five to thirty-four, about three point six percentage points a year.

The authors flag a big limit. Any visit that listed cannabis anywhere was counted, so the visit may have had another cause, such as a car accident. Nearly half of cannabis related encounters also involved another substance. The study shows trends, not cause.

Why it matters. It suggests alcohol harm is still high even as overall drinking trends down nationally, and that cannabis is increasingly present in acute care, though not always the main driver. For a Toronto listener, note this is American insurance data, so Canadian patterns may differ. The release does not say.

And finally, one thing worth trying. When you read health headlines, check three things: how many people, whether it was people or animals, and whether it shows cause or only association. All three studies today answer those questions differently.

That's your digest. It's Wednesday, November fourth. Have a good one.
