## Full script
Good morning. It's Friday, November sixth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: international news.

Three stories. Paraguay's president resigns and is impeached after a bank collapse. Tankers carrying Russian diesel change course, casting doubt on a deal announced by Washington and Moscow. And a wave of bomb threats hits flights across Europe.

Story one. A bank fails, and a president steps down.

The Associated Press reported on Thursday, November fifth, that Paraguay's president, Santiago Peña, resigned and was impeached on the same day. The lower house voted seventy-eight to zero to impeach him and sent the charges to the Senate. Congressional leaders plan to vote Friday on whether to accept his resignation. If they do, Vice President Pedro Alliana would be sworn in without a Senate trial.

The trigger was the collapse of a digital lender called Ueno Bank. The AP says Ueno grew from an obscure finance company into a household name in under five years. It operated more than seventy locations and claimed more than sixty percent of Paraguay's adults as customers. Last month it held about eight hundred million dollars in public deposits, including money from the state pension and health fund.

Regulators say that on Monday they found poorly documented loans to companies linked to the owners. The bank's capital had fallen to less than half the legal minimum. On Tuesday the central bank seized it. The app, cards and branches stopped working.

Prosecutors have opened three criminal investigations, and the attorney general's office says they cover the bank's oversight, the handling of its failure, and the placement of public money in it. A judge ordered six Ueno executives jailed pending trial. Prosecutors have charged twenty-seven bank officials and associates.

Peña said he would cooperate. In a video he said he was stepping down for, quote: "the stability of our country, social peace and the need to preserve our democratic institutions." End quote. Opposition senators argue that resigning could keep him eligible to become a senator for life, with legal protections that might block prosecution. The AP notes he held a stake in a company linked to Ueno until last year.

Here's why it matters. A state-backed deposit guarantee is now being tested. A new law requires a fund to repay depositors within five business days, and the central bank says it will fully cover about ninety-seven percent of clients. Whether that holds, and whether the resignation shields Peña, are both still open questions.

Story two. Russian diesel, and where it is going.

The AP reported Thursday that three tankers carrying Russian diesel have changed course. The shipments are tied to an arrangement President Donald Trump announced on October ninth with Russian President Vladimir Putin. It would make more than three hundred thousand metric tons of Russian diesel available to American and global markets, with larger shipments to follow. The United States had banned Russian energy imports after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in twenty twenty-two.

According to the ship trackers Kpler and MarineTraffic, two sanctioned tankers left the Baltic port of Primorsk last month, both listing the American East Coast as their destination. As of Thursday, one was headed to Brazil and the other into the Mediterranean. A third tanker in the North Atlantic changed its destination from New York to, quote, "for orders." End quote. The Financial Times first reported the diversions.

Neither the White House nor the Kremlin has said whether the deal is in writing, or what the price or payment terms are. The Treasury Department issued a sanctions exemption for Russian diesel transactions until April seventh. Treasury said it was in compliance with all applicable laws.

There is pushback. New Jersey Governor Mikie Sherrill told port and terminal operators to turn away ships carrying the fuel. A bipartisan group of senators urged Trump to cancel the deal. The administration says its authorization is lawful, and no court has ruled.

On the Russian side, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said, quote: "all the diesel will find its buyers." End quote. Putin's envoy Kirill Dmitriev called the Financial Times report fake. A Kpler analyst, Matt Smith, said regulatory and sanctions concerns mean the cargo may not be unloaded on the American Atlantic coast.

Here's why it matters. The deal was pitched as relief for record diesel prices. So far, the AP reports no confirmed delivery to the United States, and analysts doubt it would lower prices much.

Story three. Bomb threats across European skies.

The AP reported that on Thursday more than a dozen flights in Europe received bomb threats, causing emergency landings and, in some cases, military escorts. Nothing hazardous was found. British Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander called them, quote: "malicious hoax threats." End quote.

The incidents were scattered. An Aer Lingus flight from Dublin to Rome was diverted to a French military air base, after France's prime minister ordered jets to intercept it because the pilot, in his words, didn't cooperate as expected. The pilot and co-pilot were questioned. A Finnair flight to Rome was escorted by Italian jets. Three Ryanair flights bound for Sofia were affected, and one was diverted to Plovdiv. Czech police searched four planes in Prague and found nothing.

Who is behind it is unknown. An email threatening Rome-bound planes came from a group calling itself anti-feminist, and another airport said its threat used the same label. The AP says it is unclear whether they are the same group. The European Union's transport commissioner said it was too early to say whether the incidents are linked, and an information-sharing system on aviation security has been activated.

One expert, Jacob Funk Kirkegaard of the Bruegel think tank, said the threats look like stress-testing of government and airport responses. Another, Philip Baum of Coventry University, estimated each disruption could cost an airline up to two hundred and fifty thousand dollars.

Here's why it matters. Even hoaxes burn fuel, money and military resources. The AP notes some officials point to possible Russian hybrid activity behind other recent European disruptions, but it does not link these threats to Russia.

And finally, one thing worth noting. In all three stories, the key facts are still unconfirmed. Paraguay's resignation vote, the diesel deal's real destinations, and the source of the threats are all open. It's worth checking back next week.

That's your digest. It's Friday, November sixth. Have a good one.
