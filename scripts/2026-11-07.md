## Full script
Good morning. It's Saturday, November seventh, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: fascinating facts, with one theme. Honeybees.

Four facts coming up. Why honey almost never spoils. A scientist who had to take back his own theory. A bee's map of the countryside. And a new study showing that a dancing bee changes her message depending on who is watching.

Today's theme: honeybees.

Fact one. Honey, and why it keeps.

Smithsonian Magazine, in a piece by Natasha Geiling published on August twenty-second, twenty thirteen, explains the chemistry. Honey is mostly sugar, with very little water. Amina Harris of the University of California, Davis, is quoted in the piece. Quote: "Very few bacteria or microorganisms can survive in an environment like that, they just die." End quote.

There is more. Honey is acidic, with a pH of roughly three to four and a half. Harris says that acid will kill off almost anything that wants to grow there.

And the bees help. They fan nectar with their wings to drive off water. An enzyme in their stomachs, called glucose oxidase, breaks the nectar down into gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide. Harris says the peroxide goes to work against other things that could grow.

The catch is the lid. Smithsonian says honey stays unspoiled as long as it is sealed and no water gets in. Add water, or leave it in humid air, and it can spoil. The same article notes that archaeologists excavating ancient Egyptian tombs have found pots of honey thousands of years old, still preserved. Smithsonian does not say how those pots were tested, so treat that as a striking report rather than a lab result.

Fact two. A scientist who took back his own theory.

The Linda Hall Library, in a post dated June twelfth, twenty seventeen, tells the story of Karl von Frisch, the Austrian scientist known for studying how honeybees communicate. In a book in nineteen twenty-three, he proposed that a bee's round dance signaled nectar and its waggle dance signaled pollen.

The library says that theory later proved incorrect. His second and final version held that the waggle dance tells other bees the distance and direction to food. The closer the food, the faster the bees dance. And the angle of the waggle relates to the position of the sun.

The library adds two details. He worked this out during the Second World War, with funding from the Nazi Ministry of Food and Agriculture, and he had been labeled one quarter Jewish and faced possible removal from his university post. And having to retract his first theory was, in the library's words, an unpalatable episode that niggled at him for the rest of his life.

Fact three. Bees may carry a map.

Freie Universität Berlin issued a press release on March twenty-eighth, twenty twenty-three, about a study in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. Researchers tracked bees with harmonic radar. The bees wore tiny transponders that recorded their location every three seconds.

They found that a bee that watched a dance could fly to the food location from other places it already knew, not only from the hive. The researchers say bees use both the dancer's directions and their own sense of geography. Randolf Menzel, one of the authors, says the team has proven that honeybees have a map-like spatial memory.

A caution. This is a university press release, and its strong wording reflects the researchers' framing. The release does not say how many bees were tracked, so the strength of the evidence is unknown from that page.

Fact four. The dance depends on the audience.

ScienceDaily posted a summary on March twenty-fourth, twenty twenty-six, of a study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. It was led by the University of California San Diego, with the Chinese Academy of Sciences and Queen Mary University of London.

The finding is that a waggle dance is not a fixed message. Quote: "When fewer bees follow, dancers move more as they search for their audience, and the dance becomes less precise." End quote. In one experiment, the researchers varied how many bees were present. In another, they kept the number steady but added young workers, which typically do not follow dances. In both cases, dancers were less precise with a smaller or less engaged audience.

The researchers suggest bees sense their audience through touch, as followers contact the dancer with antennae and bodies. The release calls that likely, not proven. It also gives no sample sizes, and the experiments used controlled hives, so how this plays out in wild colonies is unknown.

And finally, one thing worth noticing. If you build tools that pass information along, the bees have a neat lesson. The quality of a message can depend on whether anyone is listening.

That's your digest. It's Saturday, November seventh. Have a good one.
