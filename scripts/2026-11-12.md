## Full script

Good morning. It's Thursday, November twelfth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: personal AI.

Three stories this morning. Google's phones can now make calls for you. Apple's first smart display goes on sale tomorrow, with Canada on the list. And ChatGPT is changing its voice mode and opening a front door for businesses.

Story one. Gemini picks up the phone.

Google's November Pixel Drop started rolling out on Tuesday, November tenth. The headline feature is that Gemini can now place phone calls for you, from your own phone number. Android Authority reported that it can make or change a reservation, ask a store to hold an item, or check whether something is in stock.

The same update brings Gemini task automations for a set of approved apps. Android Authority lists grocery ordering, rideshares, meal delivery, marketplace purchases and reservations. There's also a dictation tool called Rambler, which strips out filler words and turns rambling speech into clean text.

For families, there's a new Family Hub section in Settings. It gathers Google's family tools in one place. Android Authority says it's available on the Pixel six A and newer, in all Pixel markets.

Now the limits. The calling feature is in the United States only, in English, on the Pixel ten and Pixel eleven series. Android Sage, a smaller site, describes it as a public beta for users eighteen and older, with a live transcript and the option to take over the call. I couldn't confirm those details with a larger outlet, so treat them as reported, not settled. Android Authority says the rollout happens in stages, so features may take days or weeks to arrive. For Canada, the one confirmed item is notification summaries, which Android Authority says are expanding here on the Pixel nine and newer.

Here's why it matters. Phone calls are one of the last chores that still need a human voice. An assistant that can call a restaurant or a hardware store is a real time saver. For a builder, it's also a signal. The big platforms are moving from answering questions to doing errands, and the errands are being limited to approved apps. That is where the gatekeeping will happen.

Story two. Apple HomeView arrives tomorrow.

Apple's HomeView, its first smart display, goes on sale tomorrow, November thirteenth. Apple's Canadian product page confirms the date and says it is available in English to start, with Canadian French support to come. Thurrott reported that Canada is one of eight launch markets, along with the United States, the United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand, Singapore, South Africa and India. The US price is two hundred and ninety-nine dollars. I could not confirm the Canadian price on Apple's own site this morning.

The device has a seven inch screen and a twelve megapixel camera. Apple says it recognizes up to six people in a household and gives each one a personal experience. The assistant is Siri AI. Apple's footnote is clear that it is in beta.

There are real conditions. Apple's page says personal content only shows when it recognizes your face, and your paired iPhone or iPad is on the same Wi-Fi and was recently unlocked. To use Siri AI with your personal context, you need an Apple Intelligence capable iPhone or iPad on the latest software. Guests can still control smart home devices, set timers and play music. Apple also notes that some features relying on server models have daily limits, and that expanded access will cost a fee in the future. The device isn't launching in the European Union. Thurrott reported that's because Siri AI isn't available on iPhones there yet.

Here's why it matters. Apple is betting that a shared kitchen screen can tell family members apart and keep each person's information private. Face recognition plus a nearby, unlocked phone is a sensible design for a shared device. It's also a reminder that the household is becoming a multi user AI problem, not a one person chat window. And for a French speaking household in Canada, the launch version is English only.

Story three. ChatGPT changes its voice, and opens a door for businesses.

OpenAI's ChatGPT release notes for November tenth include two changes. First, OpenAI is retiring Advanced and Standard voice from ChatGPT Voice on December eleventh. The notes say that since GPT Live was introduced in July, voice usage has nearly doubled and nearly all voice users are already on it. Live video and screen sharing will stay on Advanced voice until GPT Live supports them.

Second, there's Business Hub. In OpenAI's words, quote: "Business Hub gives business owners and authorized representatives a place to claim their business in ChatGPT." End quote. After identity verification through a company called Persona, owners can update their business information, connect a product feed, and see insights such as mentions and website clicks. Eligible US ecommerce and online service businesses can also create a business agent that answers customer questions. The owner has to review and publish it before customers can chat with it.

The limits. The business agent is limited to eligible US businesses. The release notes don't say when, or whether, it comes to Canada. I read these notes through a mirror site, because OpenAI's own help page blocked my request.

Here's why it matters. If you use voice mode for hands free help in the car or kitchen, the old voices are going away in a month. And Business Hub is a new kind of listing. Just as small businesses learned to claim their map profile, they may now need to claim how an AI describes them. For the non technical founders you build for, that could become a routine setup task.

And finally, one thing worth trying. Look up how ChatGPT describes a business you know well, maybe a client's. If the answer is thin or wrong, that's a sign Business Hub, once it reaches Canada, may be worth a look.

That's your digest. It's Thursday, November twelfth. Have a good one.
