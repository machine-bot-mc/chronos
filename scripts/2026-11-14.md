## Full script
Good morning. It's Saturday, November fourteenth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: fascinating facts, with one theme. Bees.

Four facts coming up. A dance that works like a map. An insect that seems to understand nothing, in the mathematical sense. Flowers that send electric signals. And bumblebees that appear to play.

Today's theme: bees.

Fact one. The dance that gives directions.

When a honeybee scout finds food, she comes back to the hive and dances. The New World Encyclopedia, in its entry on the Austrian scientist Karl von Frisch, describes two forms. A circling dance means the food is fairly close. A more complex waggle dance means it is farther away.

The waggle dance carries real information. The angle of the dance on the comb gives the direction, measured against the sun. A waggle run straight up the comb means fly toward the sun. Other angles are read relative to that line.

Distance is in the timing. According to the encyclopedia, the waggle phase gets longer by about seventy-five milliseconds for every hundred metres to the food. And bees that have been inside the hive for a while adjust their dance angle as the sun moves across the sky.

Von Frisch's interpretation was met with scepticism when he proposed it. The encyclopedia says later work found it accurate. In nineteen seventy-three he shared the Nobel Prize with Konrad Lorenz and Nikolaas Tinbergen for their work on animal behaviour.

Fact two. Honeybees and the number zero.

Zero is a hard idea. It is not just nothing. It is nothing treated as a number, smaller than one. In June of twenty eighteen, researchers from RMIT University in Melbourne and the University of Toulouse in France reported in the journal Science that honeybees can place zero at the bottom of the number line. That's according to a news release published on EurekAlert on June seventh, twenty eighteen.

Here is how it worked. Individual bees were marked so they could be identified. They were trained to pick the image with the fewest elements on it, and were rewarded with sugar solution when they got it right. Then, in periodic tests, the bees were shown an empty image alongside one with elements on it. They chose the empty image as the lower number.

The scale of this is what surprised the team. The release says a honeybee brain has fewer than one million neurons. A human brain has around eighty-six billion. Researcher Adrian Dyer said, quote: "We've long believed only humans had the intelligence to get the concept". End quote. His colleague Aurore Avarguès-Weber said, quote: "The discovery that bees can show such elaborated understanding of numbers was really surprising given their tiny brain." End quote.

One caution. The release does not say how many bees were tested, so this is one study, in one species, and it should be read that way.

Fact three. Flowers have an electric signal.

On February twenty-first, twenty thirteen, the University of Bristol announced a study, published in Science, showing that bumblebees can detect the electric fields around flowers. The authors were Dominic Clarke, Heather Whitney, Gregory Sutton and Daniel Robert.

The physics is simple. According to Bristol, plants are typically negatively charged and give off weak electric fields. Bees pick up a positive charge as they fly. When a charged bee gets close to a charged flower, there is no spark, but a small electric force builds up between them.

The researchers found that bumblebees could detect and tell apart different floral electric fields. And in a learning test, bees learned to tell two colours apart faster when electric signals were available too.

There was one more detail. The team placed electrodes in petunia stems. When a bee landed, the flower's electrical potential changed, and stayed changed for several minutes. The researchers asked whether this might tell the next bee that a flower had just been visited. That idea was raised as a question, not tested. And at the time, Bristol said, nobody knew how bees actually sense the field. The team's guess was that the bees' hairs bristle in the electric force.

Fact four. Bumblebees that play.

In October of twenty twenty-two, a team led by Queen Mary University of London published a paper in the journal Animal Behaviour with a direct title. Do bumble bees play? The University of Oulu in Finland, whose researchers were co-authors, described the work in a news story.

In the first experiment, forty-five bumblebees were placed in an arena. They could take a clear path to food, or a path that led past some small wooden balls. Bees went to the balls and rolled them, with nothing to gain. Individual bees rolled balls anywhere from once to one hundred and seventeen times. Younger bees rolled more balls than older ones. Males rolled balls for longer than females.

In a second experiment, forty-two bees could visit two coloured chambers, one with balls and one without. Later, with no balls in either chamber, the bees preferred the colour they had linked with the balls.

The researchers concluded the ball rolling was rewarding in itself. It wasn't about finding food, clearing clutter, or mating. Co-author Kaarle Mäkelä said the behaviour was, quote: "voluntary and spontaneous - therefore akin to play behaviour as seen in other animals." End quote. First author Samadi Galpayage said, quote: "It is certainly mind-blowing, at times amusing, to watch bumble bees show something like play." End quote.

Notice the careful wording. Something like play. The study shows behaviour that meets the criteria for play. It does not prove what a bee feels.

And finally, one thing worth trying. Next spring, when bees are back in Toronto gardens, pick one honeybee on a flower and follow her for a minute. You won't see the dance, because that happens inside the hive in the dark. But you'll be watching an animal that can carry a map in its body, rank nothing below one, and sense the charge on a petal.

That's your digest. It's Saturday, November fourteenth. Have a good one.
