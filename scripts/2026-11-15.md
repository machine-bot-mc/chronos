## Full script
Good morning. It's Sunday, November fifteenth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: book reviews.

This week brought a historic Booker Prize. So today we look at the winner, and at one of the books it beat, a novel many critics had tipped to take the prize.

Book one. The End of Everything, by M. John Harrison.

On Monday, November ninth, The End of Everything won the twenty twenty-six Booker Prize. That's according to NPR's Andrew Limbong, in a report published that day. The Booker honours the best fiction published in the UK and Ireland. The winner gets fifty thousand pounds, about sixty-seven thousand US dollars, and a trophy.

Harrison is eighty-one. That makes him the oldest author ever to win the prize. This is his thirteenth novel, and he has spent most of his career writing science fiction. His two thousand six novel, Nova Swing, won both the Arthur C. Clarke Award and the Philip K. Dick Award.

The Booker Prize Foundation's chief executive, Gaby Wood, put it this way. Quote: "M. John Harrison is the first Booker winner to have emerged from the bona fide SF world." End quote.

So what is the book about? NPR describes it as a satirical science fiction novel. It follows Philip, a man who searches for artifacts along the coast of Kent, in England, and his aunt Marnie. It's set in a post-apocalyptic future, during the slow-burning invasion of shape-shifting creatures called the iGhetti. Harrison told the Booker team he drew on current affairs. Quote: "What happens when all of our expectations and explanations of the world become an ongoing disaster, but we still have to make a life?" End quote.

The judges were chaired by the historian Mary Beard. The panel also included the poet Raymond Antrobus, the Pulp frontman Jarvis Cocker, the critic Rebecca Liu, and the novelist Patricia Lockwood. Beard called it a book that, quote, "turns you upside down, raises a wry smile," end quote, and leaves you with a twinge of optimism.

Now, the critics. The review aggregator Book Marks collects six reviews. Five are raves and one is positive. There are no mixed reviews and no pans.

In The Atlantic, Hillary Kelly called it, quote, "brief and confounding." End quote. She added that it, quote, "doesn't care a whit for the conventions of dystopian fiction." End quote. Her bottom line: quote, "Really, The End of Everything is a novel about surrender." End quote.

In The New York Times Book Review, Dave Kim called it, quote, "A fascinating, sometimes frustrating and thoroughly original new novel." End quote.

In The Guardian, Michael Faber wrote that it is, quote, "Dreamlike and baffling." End quote.

There is a clear warning in these reviews, though. In the London Review of Books, Edmund Gordon notes that Harrison's novels skip the usual linear plot and dramatic resolution. His advice: quote, "There's little point in trying to make it all add up." End quote. And John Self, in The Times of London, admits he has never fully understood what was happening in a Harrison novel. Yet he says the reader is, quote, "never, ever bored." End quote.

Who would enjoy it? Readers who like their science fiction strange and literary, and who are happy to finish a book with questions rather than answers. If you want a tidy plot, the critics suggest looking elsewhere.

Book two. The Disappearers, by Marlon James.

Marlon James is the gay Jamaican writer who won the Booker in twenty fifteen for A Brief History of Seven Killings. The Disappearers was on this year's Booker shortlist alongside Harrison.

John Powers reviewed it for NPR on September twenty-fifth. He explains that the novel spans the years from nineteen eighty-eight to two thousand five, in Jamaica, during the shadow of AIDS. It's divided into five parts, each with a different tone and central figure. It begins with a group of young men in Kingston, rehearsing an openly queer play. A homophobic mob attacks the rehearsal. Most of the book follows the survivors over the years that follow, as they cope, hide, leave, or simply vanish. That's where the title comes from.

A content note. Critics agree the book contains graphic violence and graphic sex. Ron Charles notes it runs to some six hundred and sixteen pages.

Book Marks rates it a rave, based on twenty-four reviews. Some of the praise is extraordinary. In The New York Times, Dwight Garner wrote, quote, "James has written a masterpiece, the novel of the decade and possibly of the century thus far." End quote. Powers, at NPR, said, quote, "It carries you off like a tidal wave." End quote. And in The Boston Globe, Hamilton Cain called it, quote, "A harrowing, hilarious, magnificent novel that will stand the test of time." End quote.

But this book divides critics more than Harrison's. In his Substack newsletter, Ron Charles called it a monumental novel, but finished it, quote, "glad to have read it but equally glad to be done with it." End quote. In The Guardian, Yagnishsing Dawoor praised it but said the overlong narrative, quote, "sags in places." End quote. And in the London Review of Books, Adam Mars-Jones gave it a pan. He argued that, unlike Seven Killings, the new book doesn't need its great length.

Who would enjoy it? Readers who loved A Brief History of Seven Killings, and anyone ready for a long, demanding, emotionally heavy epic. Go in knowing the violence is not softened.

And finally, one thing worth trying. If you've never read M. John Harrison, The End of Everything is short, at least by the critics' account. It's an easy way to see what a Booker judging panel that included the frontman of Pulp and a historian got so excited about.

That's your digest. It's Sunday, November fifteenth. Have a good one.
