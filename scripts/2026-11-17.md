## Full script
Good morning. It's Tuesday, November seventeenth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: business.

Three deals and one regulatory fight. A chip merger worth more than a hundred billion dollars, built on light, a Canadian cannabis takeover with a hostile bidder still circling, and a railroad merger whose public comment deadline lands tomorrow.

Story one. Marvell bets on light.

On November second, Marvell Technology agreed to buy Lumentum in an all-stock deal. Light Reading reported the implied enterprise value at one hundred and fifteen billion dollars.

Here are the terms. Lumentum shareholders get four and a half Marvell shares for every Lumentum share. That works out to about one thousand one hundred and thirty-two dollars per share. Light Reading put the premium at roughly twenty-four percent, measured against October twenty-eighth, the last trading day before speculation about a deal began. After closing, Marvell shareholders would own about sixty-seven percent of the combined company, and Lumentum shareholders about thirty-three percent.

The logic is about AI data centres. Light Reading reported that AI infrastructure is moving away from separate optical parts toward tightly integrated optical systems. Marvell brings chips for interconnects, switching and custom silicon. Lumentum brings lasers, optical transceivers, optical circuit switching, and its own compound semiconductor factories.

Now the caveats. TechTarget reported on November second that the deal still needs shareholder and regulatory approval, and the companies expect it to close in the first half of twenty twenty-eight. That is a long time for a lot to change. Ron Westfall of HyperFrame Research told TechTarget the main risk is friction with Marvell's own customers, who may now see their supplier becoming a competitor. He said the deal could, quote, "disrupt Marvell's neutrality." End quote. He expects some big cloud companies and transceiver makers to shift orders to independent suppliers. He also flagged the risk of a fabless chip company taking on capital-heavy factories.

Here's why it matters. When the biggest money in tech goes into how data moves between chips, rather than the chips themselves, it tells you where the bottleneck in AI is now.

Story two. Organigram makes a friendly bid for Aurora.

On November third, the Canadian Press reported that Organigram Global agreed to buy Aurora Cannabis, valuing Aurora at five hundred and sixty-one million dollars. The report ran on BNN Bloomberg under the byline of Daniel Johnson.

Aurora shareholders can choose seven dollars and eighty cents in cash per share, five point eight six two six Organigram shares, or a mix. Total cash is capped at about two hundred and fifty-four million dollars. Both boards back the deal unanimously. The price is a ninety-three percent premium to Aurora's share price on August tenth, and twenty-one percent above the implied value of a rival offer.

That rival is Curaleaf, based in Stamford, Connecticut. In August, Curaleaf took a hostile bid straight to Aurora's shareholders, saying Aurora's board would not engage. Aurora urged shareholders to reject it. In October, Curaleaf raised its offer to about five US dollars a share in stock and cash. On the day Organigram announced its deal, Curaleaf said its bid still stands, and that it, quote, "represents better value." End quote. Curaleaf also pointed out that neither company had said whether the deal adds to or dilutes earnings.

Organigram's chief executive, James Yamanaka, called the deal, quote, "a defining step in the company's strategy to build a disciplined, global cannabis leader." End quote. The companies project more than fifty million dollars in savings and extra sales. They expect to close in January twenty twenty-seven, subject to shareholder and regulatory approval and the Supreme Court of British Columbia.

Here's why it matters. Two Canadian producers want to combine, and a US rival wants Aurora badly enough to go hostile. The companies say the prize is Aurora's manufacturing, pharmacy and patient network, paired with Organigram's Canadian brands and European footprint. Watch whether Curaleaf raises its bid again.

Story three. The fight over a coast-to-coast railroad.

Union Pacific wants to buy Norfolk Southern. FreightWaves describes it as an eighty-five billion dollar acquisition. It would create one railroad running from the West Coast to the East Coast in the United States. The deal needs approval from the Surface Transportation Board, the US rail regulator.

The calendar matters this week. In August, the board set its schedule. Formal comments on the merger, including protests and requests for conditions, are due tomorrow, November eighteenth. The railroads' responses are due February sixteenth, twenty twenty-seven. A public hearing will follow, on a date not yet set.

Opposition is organised. Progressive Railroading reported that in September, the board turned down requests from the railroad BNSF, from CSX, and from a coalition of chemical, fuel and fertilizer shipper groups to reject the application outright. But it was not a win for the merger. One board member, Richard Kloster, said the motions raised, quote, "significant, compelling arguments." End quote. Another said the railroads still had, quote, "a long way to go." End quote.

Then came a dispute over a video. FreightWaves reported that on November fourth, the board rejected Union Pacific's appeal and upheld an order that a recording of chief executive Jim Vena's speech to a shippers' conference in Houston in March must lose its confidential label. The railway CPKC, which wanted the recording made public, argued that Vena's remarks threatened higher rates for shippers who opposed the merger. That is CPKC's allegation, and FreightWaves did not independently confirm it. Union Pacific argued that public release could let others, quote, "edit, clip, manipulate, and distribute" the recording. End quote. The board said it had "serious concerns" about whether Union Pacific had influenced input from stakeholders.

Here's why it matters. The fight is now about process as much as price, and a rival railway, CPKC, is pressing hardest on it. Tomorrow's filings will show how broad the opposition really is.

And finally, one line to tie it together. All three of these deals are betting that scale wins, but each still has to get past customers, rivals and regulators before it does.

That's your digest. It's Tuesday, November seventeenth. Have a good one.
