## Full script
Good morning. It's Wednesday, November eighteenth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: health.

Three stories, all from the American Heart Association's big annual meeting in Chicago earlier this month. A heart drug that hit its target but did not help patients. A trial on whether older adults can stop taking statins. And a Brazilian trial on how low blood pressure should go after a stroke. Then one small study published today. As always, this is reporting on research, not medical advice.

Story one. A promising heart drug comes up empty.

The drug is called pelacarsen. It lowers something called lipoprotein little a, usually written L P little a. It is a cholesterol carrying particle in the blood, long suspected of raising the risk of heart disease. NPR reported on November seventh that about twenty percent of the world's population has elevated levels. That is roughly a billion people.

The trial is called L P little a HORIZON. According to the American Heart Association, it enrolled eight thousand, three hundred twenty-three adults in forty-two countries. All of them already had heart disease and high levels of this particle. They were randomly assigned to a monthly injection of the drug or a placebo, in a double blind trial. Novartis, which makes the drug, funded it. The results were published in the New England Journal of Medicine.

The drug did its chemical job. Levels fell by an average of seventy-eight percent. But there was no difference between the groups in heart attacks, strokes, urgent artery procedures or cardiovascular deaths. That held even among people with the highest levels.

There were safety signals too. Nineteen people on the drug had a severe allergic reaction called anaphylaxis, compared with none on placebo. None of those reactions were fatal. And about twenty-one percent of people on the drug stopped treatment because of side effects, compared with just over seven percent on placebo.

The senior author, Steven Nissen of the Cleveland Clinic, did not soften it. Quote: "I cannot avoid the disappointing conclusion – it didn't work." End quote.

The limits. About seventy-eight percent of participants were white, and fewer than four percent were of African ancestry. Some events during the pandemic years may have been COVID related and could not be separated out. And the experts NPR spoke to offered only theories about why it failed. Maybe the particle is a marker of risk rather than a cause. Maybe treatment has to start earlier, or last longer. UCLA cardiologist Karol Watson told NPR, quote: "The take-home message is the story's not over." End quote. Similar drugs from Amgen and Eli Lilly are still in trials.

Why it matters. This is a large, randomized trial in people, the strongest kind of evidence we have. And it is a clean lesson. Moving a number in a blood test is not the same as preventing a heart attack.

Story two. Stopping statins in older adults.

Statins are among the most prescribed drugs in the world. Many older people take them for prevention, without ever having had a heart attack or stroke. A trial presented at the same meeting asked whether they can safely stop. It was published in the New England Journal of Medicine.

According to the American Heart Association, the study included one thousand, eight hundred eighty adults aged seventy and older, in Switzerland, France and the Netherlands. Their average age was seventy-seven. None had cardiovascular disease, and all had at least two other chronic conditions. Half were assigned to stop their statin. Half kept taking it.

After two years, stopping did not significantly increase heart attacks, strokes or deaths. There were also no differences in quality of life, muscle pain or falls.

But the longer view is less tidy. At four years, twelve point seven percent of the group that stopped had a heart attack, stroke or death. In the group that continued, it was nine point two percent. The release says that later difference involved a small number of people. About fourteen percent of participants also switched groups during the study.

Study author Rita Redberg of the University of California, San Francisco, said, quote: "In general, however, discontinuation is safe for most people who are in this age group and without a previous CVD event." End quote. She also stressed shared decision making between patients and their clinicians.

Why it matters. For an older person juggling many pills, this gives real data for a conversation with a doctor. It is not a reason to stop on your own. The four year signal is exactly the kind of detail that conversation should cover.

Story three. How low should blood pressure go after a stroke?

This one comes from Brazil. The trial is called OPTIMAL-Stroke, and it was also published in the New England Journal of Medicine. It enrolled four thousand, three hundred sixty-eight people at thirty centres. All had already had a stroke or a mini stroke, and all had high blood pressure.

They were randomly assigned to one of two targets for the top number, the systolic pressure. Below one hundred twenty, or the more standard target of below one hundred forty. They were followed for an average of three point nine years.

In the intensive group, ten point nine percent had a major cardiovascular event. In the standard group, thirteen point five percent did. That works out to about twenty-one percent lower risk. Repeat strokes fell from six point four percent to four point seven percent.

Serious side effects were similar overall. But kidney related problems were more common in the intensive group. Not everyone in that group reached the target. And the researchers note the results may not carry over to countries with different health care systems. The principal investigator, Gisele Sampaio, said, quote: "we emphasize that achieving lower blood pressure safely requires appropriate medical supervision and additional monitoring". End quote.

Why it matters. Stroke survivors are at high risk of a second one. This trial adds solid evidence that a lower target can help, with the kidney trade off as the main caution.

And finally, one small study published today. In the journal Obesity, French and American researchers randomly assigned thirty sedentary adults with overweight or obesity to forty-five minutes of brisk walking, five days a week, for six weeks. One group did it as short bouts spread through the day. The other did it all at once. The short bouts group had better blood sugar control, according to a Wiley release. It is thirty people, and the release gives no numbers. So treat it as a hint, not a rule.

That's your digest. It's Wednesday, November eighteenth. Have a good one.
