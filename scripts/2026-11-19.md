## Full script
Good morning. It's Thursday, November nineteenth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: personal AI.

Three stories from the past week. Apple puts its new Siri on a screen for the whole family. Microsoft wants Copilot to prepare your workday before you ask. And ChatGPT gets a wallet so its agents can finish a purchase for you.

Story one. Apple HomeView brings Siri AI into the kitchen.

Apple's newsroom said on November thirteenth that HomeView is available to order. It's a home device with a seven-inch display, in two models: one that sits on a table, and one that mounts on a wall. Each is two hundred and ninety-nine US dollars. Apple's page doesn't give a separate Canadian price. Apple also lists optional power mounts from Schneider Home starting at fifty-nine dollars and ninety-five cents, US.

It runs what Apple calls an entirely new version of Siri, named Siri AI. Apple says it can see what's on screen and take actions across apps. Paired with a supported iPhone or iPad, it can use personal context like your calendar and messages.

The family part is the interesting bit. HomeView recognizes up to six people by face. When it sees you, it shows your own messages, calendar, reminders and Find My. It also handles music, FaceTime calls, home monitoring and hands-free automations through the Home app.

Now the limits. Siri AI is labelled beta on this device. Apple says personal content appears, quote, "only when a user is recognized by face and their paired iPhone or iPad is present and recently unlocked." End quote. So each person needs their own compatible phone or tablet. Apple also says some server-side features have daily usage limits, and that expanded access will cost a fee in the future. It didn't say how much.

For Canada, the news is good. Canada is on the list of countries that can order, and Siri AI works in English here. Apple says Canadian French will come in a later free update. It isn't sold in the European Union, because Siri AI isn't available there.

Here's why it matters. A shared screen that knows who's standing in front of it is a different design problem from a personal phone. Anyone building family tools should watch how Apple handles one device, six people and private data.

Story two. Copilot Today, an assistant that gets ready before you do.

At its Ignite conference on November seventeenth, Microsoft laid out a batch of Copilot changes. That's from a post on Microsoft's Copilot blog by Jared Spataro, the company's chief marketing officer for Copilot.

The headline for daily work is a feature called Today. Microsoft describes it as a command center across your mail, calendar, Teams chats, meetings and tasks. It flags what you missed and what needs attention. Then it prepares work for you to review, such as a draft reply, a proposed schedule change or a pending follow-up.

Two other pieces are now generally available. Home puts chat and Cowork, Microsoft's tool for handing off multistep jobs, in one starting place. And Office in Copilot lets you go from an idea to a finished Word document, Excel workbook or PowerPoint deck.

Microsoft also introduced Autopilot, an agent with its own identity that you can mention in Teams or Outlook. It can follow up on email, run recurring tasks and manage projects. Microsoft says you can approve key actions before they happen.

Now the caveats. Today is only in private preview, in the Copilot app and in Outlook. Autopilot is just starting to roll out to Microsoft's early-access Frontier customers. The post also claims Copilot is much cheaper than rival assistants, but those numbers come from a report Microsoft cites, so treat them as vendor claims.

Here's why it matters. The pattern is shifting from you asking and the assistant answering, to the assistant preparing and you approving. If you build tools for founders, that review-and-approve step is where trust is won or lost.

Story three. ChatGPT Wallet lets agents check out for you.

TechCrunch reported on November thirteenth that OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet. ChatGPT's agents, which OpenAI calls dots, can now help you pick items and then complete the purchase.

You add a card once. When you ask for help with a purchase, you review the items, the total and the card, then approve with a check like Face ID or a fingerprint. TechCrunch says the payment goes through a token that stands in for your card details, so neither the agent nor the store sees your card number. OpenAI says you keep card rewards and normal protections, like disputing a charge.

The limits are big. It's only for a limited group of US Pro subscribers, eighteen or older, with a US-issued Visa, Mastercard or American Express. So it's not available in Canada for now. The article doesn't name which stores work. It also notes some retailers block AI agents, and that Amazon is the largest to ban them.

OpenAI plans to add spending limits and rules by category or time period. That would let you pre-approve some purchases, so the agent could act more on its own.

Here's why it matters. Paying is the last step most agents couldn't take. Once it's safe to hand over, chores like reordering household basics start to look automatable. But the store list, and who's allowed to buy what, will decide how useful it really is.

And finally, one thing worth trying. Look at one recurring task in your week, like a weekly grocery order or a Monday inbox sweep. Write down which step you'd want to approve yourself and which you'd happily hand off. All three companies are betting that this split is where personal AI lives.

That's your digest. It's Thursday, November nineteenth. Have a good one.
