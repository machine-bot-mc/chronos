## Full script
Good morning. It's Friday, November twentieth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: international news.

Three stories this week. The leaders of the United States, China and Russia meet as a trio for the first time. The world's top court finds Myanmar responsible for genocide against the Rohingya. And Iran widens its attacks in the Gulf, to a US air base in Kuwait and sites in Bahrain.

Story one. Three leaders in Shenzhen.

Al Jazeera reported on Thursday, November nineteenth, that Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin held their first three-way meeting. It took place in Shenzhen, China, on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit, known as APEC. The meeting ran more than an hour, and ended with a joint declaration.

According to Al Jazeera, the statement covers four themes. Stable energy flows. A dialogue on reducing nuclear risk and stopping the spread of nuclear weapons. Strategic stability and international security. And a warning to the International Criminal Court. The declaration demanded that the court and similar institutions not, quote: "abuse their positions." End quote. None of the three countries is a member of that court, and the court has issued an arrest warrant for Putin.

The leaders framed it in large terms. Trump said, quote: "We're making history." End quote. Before the meeting, Xi said, quote: "Nuclear war cannot be won and must not be fought; every effort must be made to prevent it." End quote. Afterward, Putin called the discussion very frank.

There are real limits. Al Jazeera notes the full text was not published, and it's unclear how the energy goals would be met. A Kremlin spokesperson said Ukraine was not the central topic. Iran came up only in Trump's statement that Tehran cannot have nuclear weapons. And while the three met, European leaders gathered in Berlin to discuss support for Ukraine.

There is a Canadian angle. CTV News reported from Shenzhen on November eighteenth that Prime Minister Mark Carney met Trump at the summit. The Canadian readout said, quote: "The leaders discussed the work underway to reach a mutually beneficial trade agreement." End quote. Carney also met Xi the day before. And he rejected Putin's claim that sanctions over Ukraine no longer matter, saying sanctions are going to continue to increase.

Why it matters. Three rival powers signed one document. But with no full text published, it's hard to say what it commits anyone to. For now, it reads as a statement of shared interests, not a plan.

Story two. Myanmar and the Rohingya.

UN News reported that on Thursday, November nineteenth, the International Court of Justice in The Hague ruled that Myanmar is responsible for genocide against the Rohingya. The UN says it's the first time the court has found a state responsible for genocide. Gambia brought the case in November twenty nineteen, under the Genocide Convention.

The court found that Myanmar's security forces and other state bodies killed members of the group, caused serious bodily or mental harm, and imposed measures to prevent births. It also found Myanmar failed to prevent and punish genocide. According to UN News, about seven hundred thousand Rohingya fled to Bangladesh in twenty seventeen. The court estimated about ten thousand were killed. Nearly four hundred villages were partly or fully destroyed.

Myanmar has been ordered to investigate and prosecute suspects, allow a safe and voluntary return for displaced Rohingya, and pay compensation. It must report back within two years. UN Secretary-General António Guterres called the judgment a, quote: "powerful recognition of the Rohingya victims of genocide." End quote.

Myanmar rejects it. Reuters reported on Friday that government spokesperson Khaing Khaing Soe said, quote: "Our country was unjustly accused." End quote. She pointed out the decision was not unanimous. And she echoed the Shenzhen declaration, saying major world leaders had warned that bodies like this court should not abuse their judicial authority.

Why it matters. The ruling is final and cannot be appealed. But the court has no way to enforce it. Reuters reports thousands of refugees in Bangladesh's camps celebrated, yet it's unclear when they can go home.

Story three. Iran widens its Gulf attacks.

This follows our October twenty-third episode on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz, with new developments. The Associated Press reported on Wednesday, November eighteenth, that Iran targeted Ali Al Salem, a US air base in Kuwait. Iran's state broadcaster said its forces fired at air defense systems and drone and missile launch platforms. Kuwait's military said it intercepted the drones, with no immediate damage. Iran also launched several attacks on US facilities in Bahrain, home of the US Navy's Fifth Fleet.

The two sides tell it differently. The US said a strike the day before, on a Revolutionary Guard missile site on Qeshm Island, was retaliation for an Iranian attack on an American radar in Bahrain. Iran said its attacks answer US drone surveillance and an attack on Iranian communications towers. Kuwait's Foreign Ministry called the repeated attacks criminal.

What isn't known. No damage or casualties were confirmed for Wednesday's attacks, and the US military did not immediately comment. The status of diplomatic talks is unclear. The war began on February twenty-eighth, when Israel and the US attacked Iran, and a US naval blockade is still in place.

Analyst Hamidreza Azizi of the International Crisis Group wrote that, quote: "the accumulation of military incidents across multiple fronts increases the risk of miscalculation." End quote.

Why it matters. Each strike is small on its own. Together they widen the map, and make a deal harder to reach.

One thread ties this week together. An international court spoke with new force on Thursday. On the same day, three of the world's most powerful governments warned courts like it to stay in their lane.

That's your digest. It's Friday, November twentieth. Have a good one.
