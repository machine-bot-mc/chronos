## Full script
Good morning. It's Saturday, November twenty-first, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: fascinating facts, with one theme. Tardigrades.

Coming up: an animal that went to space without a suit, a protein that shields human cells, a thirty-year nap in the Antarctic freezer, and what happens when you fire one out of a gun.

Today's theme: tardigrades.

Tardigrades are often called water bears. According to a twenty oh eight report on ScienceDaily, they are between a tenth of a millimetre and one and a half millimetres long. So you would need a microscope to see one. What makes them famous is how much punishment they can take, especially when they are dried out or frozen into a dormant state.

Fact one. The first animals tested in open space.

In September twenty oh seven, the European Space Agency launched a spacecraft called FOTON-M3 into low Earth orbit, around two hundred and seventy kilometres up. On board were dried-out tardigrades, exposed directly to space. The study was led by Ingemar Jönsson of Kristianstad University in Sweden, and published in the journal Current Biology in September twenty oh eight.

ScienceDaily reported the result on September ninth, twenty oh eight. Quote: "This is the first time that any animal has been tested for survival under open-space conditions." End quote. Most of the tardigrades survived the vacuum and cosmic rays alone. Some even survived solar ultraviolet radiation more than a thousand times stronger than at Earth's surface. And the survivors could still reproduce after the trip.

There is a limit worth noting. Only some survived the full ultraviolet dose, and the researchers said how they repaired that damage remained a mystery.

Fact two. A tardigrade protein that protects human cells.

In twenty sixteen, a team at the University of Tokyo read the genome of one especially tough tardigrade species, Ramazzottius varieornatus. They found a protein that exists only in tardigrades. They named it Dsup, short for damage suppressor.

The University of Tokyo described the experiment on September twenty-first, twenty sixteen. Ordinary human cells grown in a lab were hit with X-rays, and their DNA was damaged. Then the researchers gave the same kind of human cells the ability to make the Dsup protein. Quote: "they showed approximately half the DNA damage." End quote. And, as the university put it, most surprisingly, those cells were still capable of reproducing.

The caveat is important. This was done in human cells in a dish, not in people. It shows a protective effect in the lab. It does not mean anyone can be made radiation-proof. The study, led by Takuma Hashimoto and Takekazu Kunieda, appeared in the journal Nature Communications.

Fact three. Frozen for thirty years, then back to life.

In November nineteen eighty-three, a sample of moss was collected in Antarctica and stored in a freezer at minus twenty degrees Celsius. It stayed there for more than thirty years. In May twenty fourteen, researchers at Japan's National Institute of Polar Research thawed it out and soaked it in water.

ScienceDaily reported the results on February sixteenth, twenty sixteen. The team found two tiny tardigrades, each about a fifth of a millimetre long, and one egg. One adult slowly recovered. It took about two weeks before it could move and feed, and it went on to lay nineteen eggs, fourteen of which hatched. The second adult never fully recovered, and died twenty days after being rehydrated. The egg hatched, and that youngster grew up and laid fifteen eggs of its own.

Before this, the longest recorded tardigrade survival was about eight or nine years. The slow recovery suggests that thirty years on ice did cause some damage. The lead researcher, Megumu Tsujimoto, said the team now aims to understand how these animals survive so long. The study appeared in the journal Cryobiology.

Fact four. Tough, but not indestructible.

In April twenty nineteen, an Israeli lunar probe called Beresheet, built by SpaceIL and Israel Aerospace Industries, crashed into the Moon. According to the University of Arizona's astrobiology program, tardigrades were part of its payload. That raised a natural question. Could they have survived?

Researchers at the University of Kent decided to test it. As the university reported on May twenty-first, twenty twenty-one, Alejandra Traspas and Professor Mark Burchell froze tardigrades into a dormant state, loaded them into nylon bullets, and fired them from a gas gun into sand. The university said they survived impacts up to about nine hundred metres per second. Faster than that, the animals were destroyed. Traspas summed it up this way. Quote: "This research shows that there are limits to the survivability of tardigrades". End quote. The work also tests a bigger idea, that life could hitch a ride between planets on debris from impacts. Her conclusion was that typical impact speeds would not let animals like this survive the trip.

Science News, in a June twenty twenty-one article, put the limit a little lower, at about eight hundred and twenty-five metres per second. Survivors also took up to thirty-six hours to recover, compared with nine hours for animals that were frozen but never fired.

As for the Moon, Traspas Muiña told Science News the Beresheet tardigrades probably did not survive the landing. And the University of Arizona points out that even if some did, there is no liquid water, oxygen or food there, so they could never wake up, let alone reproduce. Quote: "Their colonizing the moon is thus impossible." End quote.

There is one more caveat. The gun experiment tested just one species. A researcher not involved in the study, Jason Pienaar, noted that there are much tougher tardigrades out there.

And finally, one thing worth trying. Every tardigrade in today's Antarctic story came out of a clump of moss soaked in water. If you have a microscope at home, a soaked bit of moss is a fine place to go looking for a water bear of your own.

That's your digest. It's Saturday, November twenty-first. Have a good one.
