## Full script
Good morning. It's Sunday, November twenty-second, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: book reviews.

Two new novels today. One just won Canada's biggest fiction prize, and it ends up on a road trip to Toronto. The other is a Cold War spy story, written by the son of John le Carre, and it brings back George Smiley.

Book one. Your Breath in Charcoal, by Nicholas Herring.

Last Monday night, November sixteenth, this novel won the twenty twenty-six Giller Prize. The Canadian Press reported that the prize is worth one hundred thousand dollars, and was presented at the Ritz-Carlton here in Toronto. Rick Mercer hosted, for the sixth time. The winner was chosen from one hundred and forty books submitted from across the country.

Herring lives in Murray Harbour, Prince Edward Island. He also works as a carpenter and a fisherman. His first novel, Some Hellish, won the Atwood Gibson Writers' Trust Fiction Prize. The Canadian Press says that award let him take ten months off work, and that he wrote this second novel in six months.

There was something new about the jury this year, too. For the first time, booksellers sat on it. It was a nine-member panel, including people from Ben McNally Books in Toronto and McNally Robinson Booksellers. One of them, Chris Hall, said booksellers are, Quote: "looking for somebody who's telling us something about the world we didn't already know." End quote.

So what is the book about? It was published on September first by Goose Lane Editions, and runs three hundred and twenty-eight pages. The publisher's description opens like this. Quote: "Knickle is a young Islander with no friends, direction, or purpose in his life." End quote. Knickle drives to northern New Brunswick to pick up a friend's brother from a mental health hospital. Then the two of them, on impulse, keep heading west, to Toronto. That's as far as we'll go with the plot.

Now, what the critics said. The Giller jury called it, Quote: "a deeply philosophical narrative filled with pop culture references, literary allusions, and mordant, quotable witticisms." End quote. That was reported by the Canadian Press. In its citation, quoted by the publisher, the jury also called it, Quote: "a riotously funny working-class odyssey filled with love, loss, and profound hope." End quote.

In the Literary Review of Canada, on October twentieth, the critic Christina Apa noted that the novel is set in the nineteen nineties. She called it, Quote: "a deeply immersive novel." End quote. And she concluded, Quote: "Your Breath in Charcoal is a moving story of demanding more from the life one is given." End quote.

One caution. Beyond the jury and the Literary Review of Canada, I couldn't find reviews from major Canadian papers on pages I could read this morning. So treat this as early reception, not a settled verdict.

Who would enjoy it? Readers who like a funny, talkative narrator, a working-class Maritime voice, and a road novel that ends up in our own city.

Book two. The Taper Man, by Nick Harkaway.

Nick Harkaway's real name is Nicholas Cornwell. He is the son of John le Carre, the former British intelligence officer who wrote the George Smiley novels. That's according to an Associated Press feature published on November fourteenth. Harkaway first made his name with science fiction thrillers. His first le Carre novel, Karla's Choice, came out in twenty twenty-four. This is his second.

The Taper Man came out in the United States on Tuesday, November seventeenth, from Viking.

Here is the setup, from the publisher, Penguin Random House. It's nineteen sixty-five. The Circus, which is le Carre's name for British intelligence, has a valuable source inside the Red Army in Moscow. Then a British agent is shot on a routine trip to Helsinki. Smiley discovers a counterintelligence operation that threatens that source. The trail leads to Los Angeles, where Smiley's Soviet rival, Karla, may have a deep-cover agent, the Taper Man of the title. Meanwhile, Roy Bland, a Circus agent in Leningrad, takes serious risks on the Soviet side. The Associated Press notes that the story is set two years after The Spy Who Came in from the Cold, and that it also brings back Control.

Harkaway told the Associated Press that writing a Cold War story in twenty twenty-six is like, Quote: "writing in the mirror." End quote.

Now, what the critics said. The review aggregator Book Marks lists seven reviews. By its count, five are raves, one is positive, and one is mixed. No pans.

Alice Cary, in BookPage, wrote, Quote: "Cerebral, clever and taut, The Taper Man is another George Smiley thriller well worth diving into." End quote. Stuart Kelly, in The Scotsman, called it, Quote: "undiluted delight." End quote. Publishers Weekly said, Quote: "Harkaway exhibits his father's knack for slow reveals, complex storylines, and salient observations about human nature." End quote. And it added, Quote: "George Smiley is in good hands." End quote.

Ron Charles, writing on his Substack, called it, Quote: "Terrific fun." End quote. He also said Harkaway, Quote: "exposes the deep-seated prejudices that infected America's intelligence establishment." End quote.

The one mixed review came from Kirkus, which called it a densely plotted installment, and suggested not every reader will keep up with every turn.

Who would enjoy it? Anyone who loved Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy and wants more Smiley. If you've never read le Carre, the critics' warnings about the dense plot are worth keeping in mind.

And finally, one thing worth trying. The Giller shortlist makes an easy reading list for the winter. Alongside the winner, it includes Eliza Sunshine by Michelle Good, The Small Ones by Nazanine Hozar, Wild People Quiet by Tara Gereaux, and Good People by Patmeena Sabit.

That's your digest. It's Sunday, November twenty-second. Have a good one.
