## Full script
Good morning. It's Tuesday, November twenty-fourth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: business.

Three stories about consolidation and control. An Alberta gas deal that shows where Canadian energy money is going, a Hollywood studio merger that is now reshaping itself from the inside, and Google putting a checkout button inside its AI, with Canadian retailers first in line.

Story one. Canadian Natural buys Advantage Energy.

On November sixteenth, Canadian Natural Resources agreed to buy Advantage Energy for thirteen dollars and fifty-five cents a share, in cash. The Canadian Press, writing on BNN Bloomberg that day, put the total value at two point five three billion dollars, including debt.

Bloomberg Law reported the price was a twenty-five percent premium over Advantage's closing price the Friday before. The equity alone is worth about two point two five billion dollars.

What Canadian Natural gets is production. About seventy-nine thousand barrels of oil equivalent per day, from the Montney and Charlie Lake areas of northwestern Alberta. Those lands sit right next to Canadian Natural's existing operations around Grande Prairie, and they include undeveloped light oil and liquids-rich natural gas.

Canadian Natural's president, Scott Stauth, said, quote, "Advantage Energy is an excellent strategic fit for Canadian Natural." End quote. Advantage's board chair and interim chief executive, John Festival, said the board concluded the deal is, quote, "the best path to crystallize the value we have created." End quote.

Now the caveats. Desjardins analyst Robert Mann told The Canadian Press that Canadian Natural, quote, "seems to be paying full price for Advantage," end quote, which he called somewhat against the grain for a company that usually buys when prices are low. He said the Competition Act review is the main milestone to watch, because some of Canadian Natural's recent deals have been delayed there. The deal also needs Advantage shareholders to approve it, and it is targeted to close in the first quarter of twenty twenty-seven.

Bloomberg Law framed the deal as part of a run of Canadian oil and gas takeovers. It said conflicts in the Middle East and Ukraine are boosting demand for alternative supplies.

Here's why it matters. Canadian Natural is paying with cash on hand and its bank credit lines, and it says it still aims to return all of its free cash flow through share buybacks. Big Canadian producers are choosing to buy proven production next door rather than build something new. That is a statement about how they see the next few years.

Story two. Hollywood's new giant starts rearranging the furniture.

Some background first. On October sixth, Paramount closed its eighty-one billion dollar takeover of Warner Bros. Discovery, as the Associated Press reported that day. Including debt, the AP said, the deal comes to nearly one hundred and eleven billion dollars. The combined company is now called Skydance, led by David Ellison, with Ynon Kreiz as co-chief executive.

Now the new part. On November eighteenth, the Associated Press reported that Skydance will merge its two big television production arms. Warner Bros. Television Group and Paramount Television Studios become one company, called Warner Bros. Television Studios. It will hold more than one hundred and twenty series, including The Pitt, Ted Lasso, Reacher and Landman. Channing Dungey and Matt Thunell will serve as presidents. CBS Studios, which makes NCIS, stays separate.

In an internal memo, Skydance TV's George Cheeks said, quote, "Bringing together two of the industry's most prolific television studios creates a formidable creative engine." End quote. The studio merger is expected to be done in early twenty twenty-seven.

The caveats. The AP report did not mention job cuts, and I won't guess at any. But when the takeover closed, the AP noted that thousands of industry figures had signed an open letter in April warning of fewer jobs and less choice for audiences. Twelve state attorneys general had sued to block the deal, then settled in September with commitments on film production and a fund for displaced workers. Critics said those remedies were too weak.

Here's why it matters. Big media mergers are announced as one number. The real changes come months later, as divisions are folded together. One studio now makes shows for HBO, Netflix and CBS at the same time. That gives it a lot of leverage over the people who sell to it, and the people who buy from it.

Story three. Google puts a buy button inside its AI, in Canada.

On November fourth, Google Canada announced that shoppers can now buy some products directly inside AI Mode in Google Search and inside the Gemini app, without going to the store's website. The first retailers are lululemon, Shoppers Drug Mart, Simons, and select Shopify merchants. That was reported the same day by The Canadian Press and in a post by Sabrina Geremia, who runs Google Canada.

Here's how it works. You ask for something with specific features, style or colour. You pick from the results, and pay with Google Pay, using the card and shipping address already saved in your Google Wallet. Google's post says, quote, "Retailers remain the seller of record." End quote.

Under the hood is something called the Universal Commerce Protocol, an open standard Google and partners launched earlier this year. It gives AI agents a shared way to find products, buy them, and handle what happens after the sale.

The limits. Only a handful of retailers are in for now. Google says that, quote, "Starting next year," end quote, it will open a simpler way for other retailers to connect through its Merchant Center. Features like loyalty rewards are still to come. Google also says fifty-nine percent of Canadian holiday shoppers plan to use an AI tool this season, but that figure comes from Google's own research, which it doesn't describe. The Canadian Press noted that OpenAI and Meta are adding similar checkout features to their own AI platforms.

Here's why it matters, especially for a builder. If the customer never visits the store's site, the store loses the page where it upsells, collects email addresses and learns about its buyers. The AI becomes the shop window. For anyone building for small merchants, getting their products readable by these agents may soon matter as much as having a good website.

And finally, one thing worth trying. Next time you shop online, ask Gemini or Google's AI Mode for something specific from a Canadian store, and see whether the buy button shows up. Notice what information you'd be handing over, and what the store never sees.

That's your digest. It's Tuesday, November twenty-fourth. Have a good one.
