## Full script
Good morning. It's Wednesday, November twenty-fifth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: health.

We have a light and sound device for Alzheimer's that missed its main goal, a new injection that sharply cuts triglycerides, and a small diet trial that pits keto against the Mediterranean diet.

Story one. A flickering light for Alzheimer's falls short.

The HOPE trial tested a device called Spectris, made by Cognito Therapeutics. People wear it at home for one hour a day. It plays light and sound that pulse forty times a second. The results were presented on November sixteenth at the Clinical Trials on Alzheimer's Disease conference in Boston, as reported by NeurologyLive.

This was a large trial in people. Six hundred and seventy-three adults with confirmed Alzheimer's disease, mild to moderate, took part for twelve months. It was randomized and double-blind, and it compared the real device with a sham version.

The headline result is negative. On the main measure, a combined score of memory and daily functioning, the device did not beat the sham.

The company is pointing to a smaller group. Among three hundred and ninety-five people with mild disease, the device was linked to fifty-six percent slower decline in daily activities, and forty-seven percent slower decline on a standard dementia rating scale. But NeurologyLive notes that these subgroup numbers were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. The results had been presented at a conference and described in a company release, and the article gives no sign they have been peer reviewed.

On safety, seizures were reported in under one percent of people on the device, against a third of a percent on sham. Headaches and ringing in the ears were also slightly more common.

Here's why it matters. A cheap, at-home device with no drug side effects would be a big deal. But a missed main goal plus a promising subgroup is a signal to test again, not proof. The device is not authorized in the United States, and the company plans to talk to the FDA next.

Story two. A new shot for high triglycerides.

Triglycerides are a type of fat in the blood. High levels are tied to heart disease and, when very high, to inflammation of the pancreas. A trial called TYDAL TIMI seventy-eight tested an antibody called MAR zero zero one, from Marea Therapeutics. It was presented on November ninth at the American Heart Association meeting and published the same day in the New England Journal of Medicine, as reported by TCTMD.

Two hundred and thirty-six adults with raised triglycerides were randomized to one of three doses or a placebo, injected every four weeks for twenty-four weeks.

At twelve weeks, triglycerides fell by roughly thirty-nine to forty-six percent compared with placebo, depending on the dose. In people who started with very high levels, the drop was as large as seventy-two percent. Fewer people on the drug had side effects than on placebo, and there were no deaths and no cases of pancreatitis. LDL cholesterol, the so-called bad cholesterol, rose slightly.

Now the limits. This is a small, short trial, funded by the company, and it measured blood levels, not heart attacks or strokes. Experts at the meeting were cautious. Doctor Marc Bonaca said lowering these fats is important, but quote: "translating that into outcomes is hard." End quote. Another discussant noted that triglyceride lowering is not a proven stand-in for fewer heart events. A larger, longer trial would be needed to show real benefit.

Here's why it matters. This antibody blocks a protein called ANGPTL four, a different target from today's common cholesterol drugs. Its clearest near-term use may be people with extremely high triglycerides, where the risk of pancreatitis is real. A second part of the trial is now testing exactly that group.

Story three. Keto versus Mediterranean, when weight loss is equal.

This one is a few months old but worth knowing. A randomized trial from Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis was published on August twenty-seventh in the journal Cell Metabolism, and described by ScienceDaily two days later.

Forty-two adults with obesity, prediabetes and fatty liver were put on one of three diets. One was ketogenic, very low in carbohydrates. One was Mediterranean. One was low fat and plant forward. The researchers supplied all the food, and adjusted calories so that everyone lost about ten percent of their body weight. That took about four to five months.

Everyone improved. Body fat fell and insulin sensitivity rose in all three groups. But some differences stood out. Liver fat fell by an average of sixty-seven percent on keto, versus forty-five percent on each of the other two diets. And about half the keto group no longer met the criteria for prediabetes, compared with twenty-nine percent on the Mediterranean diet and seven percent on the plant forward diet. The researchers said blood fats and cholesterol did not rise on keto, though the summary gave no numbers.

The limits are big. Forty-two people is small, split three ways. All the food was provided, which is not how real life works. And four to five months tells us nothing about the long term. This is a comparison in people, with random assignment, but it is one early study.

Here's why it matters. In this trial, losing weight helped, whichever of the three diets people followed. The composition of the diet may add something on top, especially for liver fat. Researcher Max Petersen put it this way. Quote: "our study shows that the specific composition of the diet can give you added benefits." End quote. It's a reason for more research, not a reason to change your plate without talking to a doctor.

And finally, a thread that ties today together. All three stories show a gap between a promising number and a proven result. A subgroup, a blood test, a short trial. Each is a step, not the finish line.

That's your digest. It's Wednesday, November twenty-fifth. Have a good one.
