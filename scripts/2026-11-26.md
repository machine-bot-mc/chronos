## Full script
Good morning. It's Thursday, November twenty-sixth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: personal AI.

Three stories from the past week. Google lets its search AI read your wallet and your documents. Meta puts its Muse assistant inside Instagram messages, and Canada is included. And a new study checks how well AI agents handle everyday life. The answer is: not well enough yet.

Story one. Google's AI Mode gets to know your finances.

On November twenty-third, Google's Robby Stein, vice president of product for Search, wrote on the company blog that Personal Intelligence in Search's AI Mode is connecting to more apps. It already worked with Gmail, Calendar and Photos. Google Wallet is rolling out first, starting that day. Google Drive and the Gemini app are coming in the coming weeks.

What does that look like? With Wallet, Google's examples are mapping out financial milestones, finding ways to cut everyday spending, and getting more out of credit card cash-back categories, travel perks and reward points. With Drive, AI Mode can pull from your docs, spreadsheets and PDFs. Google's example is a personal plan to get ready for tax season.

There's also shared memory. If you opt in, ideas you explored in the Gemini app carry over to AI Mode, and the other way around. Google says you can correct what it knows, for example by telling it you're no longer vegetarian.

Now the limits. Everything here is opt-in, and you can switch connections off at any time. On money, Google is clear. Quote: "AI Mode can only read information from your accounts; it can't move, manage, or invest your money." End quote. Google says a Personalization icon will soon show what context was used for an answer. The blog doesn't say which countries get this, which plans it needs, or whether Canada is included. So for now, treat it as unconfirmed here.

Here's why it matters. The big assistants are racing to own your personal context. Mail, calendar, photos, and now money and files. Whoever holds that context gives the most useful answers. For a builder, the lesson is in the design: read-only access first, and a visible receipt of what the AI looked at.

Story two. Muse moves into your Instagram messages.

TechCrunch's Aisha Malik reported on November twenty-fourth that Meta is bringing its Muse AI assistant directly into Instagram direct messages. It rolls out on iOS and Android in the US, Canada, Mexico, Japan and Singapore.

The features are practical. Muse can give more context about a post, place or product you come across. It can catch you up on conversations you missed in other chats. And it can act on posts people share with you. TechCrunch's examples are adding a coffee shop to a trip itinerary, or finding a Black Friday deal on a pair of shoes.

The most practical part may be saved posts. Muse can sort your bookmarked posts and reels into folders, turn a recipe video into a grocery list, turn travel videos into an itinerary, or suggest outfits from saved fashion reels. When you save something, you'll get a prompt offering to organize it. That prompt is only launching in the US and Canada.

On privacy, Meta says, quote: "Nothing is shared, published, or sent without their approval." End quote. Users can also ask Muse to forget information or undo actions. The article doesn't cover pricing, usage limits or age requirements.

The same day, TechCrunch also reported that Walmart is letting Muse users find deals, compare products and build a cart in the Muse app. Shoppers can set spending limits and require approval before a purchase. That article doesn't say whether it works in Canada.

Here's why it matters. Many people's to-do lists already live in their saved posts. A recipe, a restaurant, a gift idea. Turning that pile into a grocery list or a plan is exactly the kind of small, daily automation that sticks. And because Canada is in the first wave, it's something you can actually try.

Story three. A reality check on AI agents.

Also on November twenty-fourth, TechCrunch's Rebecca Bellan reported on a new benchmark called ShellBench. It tests AI agents on everyday, multi-step personal tasks. It was built with researchers from The OpenClaw Foundation, Parsewave, Hugging Face, Red Hat and UC Berkeley, and it draws on more than ten million anonymized, real agent interactions.

The results are sobering. Even the most advanced models failed more than a third of the time. Across all models, about sixteen percent of attempts broke at least one safety requirement. Consistency was weak too. When the top model, OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, ran the same task three times, it passed all three attempts less than half the time.

The specific failures sound like family life. On a task about a child's school pickup, almost eighty percent of attempts failed. On avoiding duplicate or wrong invoice payments, about forty percent of attempts recommended them anyway.

The study also found that the software wrapped around a model matters a lot. Among the stronger models, results depended more on that wrapper than on the model itself. One Meta model scored fifty-six percent in one setup and thirty-eight percent in another.

There are caveats. The researchers didn't test these models inside the companies' own consumer apps, like Muse. So the results may not match what you'd see on your phone. And the article doesn't say how many tasks the benchmark includes.

Here's why it matters. The first two stories are about agents getting more access to your life. This one says they still drop the ball, often. For anyone building for non-technical founders, the takeaway is simple. Keep a human approval step on anything involving money, kids or schedules, and design so the agent's mistakes are easy to catch.

And finally, one thing worth trying. If you use Instagram, save a recipe reel and ask Muse to turn it into a grocery list. Then check it against the video. That's a two-minute test of how far these assistants have really come.

That's your digest. It's Thursday, November twenty-sixth. Have a good one.
