Good morning. It's Friday, November twenty-seventh, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: international news.

Coming up: Ebola in eastern Congo passes twelve thousand confirmed cases, a US jury convicts Huawei and Beijing pushes back, and Europe's leaders walk away from a budget summit without a deal.

Story one. Ebola passes twelve thousand cases in Congo.

The Ebola outbreak in eastern Congo has now passed twelve thousand confirmed cases. That's according to Congo's Ministry of Health, in figures released on Thursday and reported by the Associated Press. The exact count is twelve thousand and twenty-one. Nearly half of those confirmed cases have died.

The AP calls it the fastest-growing Ebola outbreak in history. It is the deadliest of Congo's seventeen outbreaks, and it is on track to surpass the twenty fourteen to twenty sixteen epidemic in West Africa, which killed about eleven thousand people.

What makes this one so hard is the virus itself. It's the rare Bundibugyo strain, and the AP reports there is no approved vaccine or treatment for it. The outbreak was declared in May.

And the fighting is making it worse. Clashes between the army and a rebel group called the CRP resumed on Thursday in a mining town about twenty-five kilometres north of Bunia, the provincial capital and a main hub of the Ebola response. On Tuesday, about a dozen people, including two World Health Organization staff, were evacuated after three days trapped in a hospital amid crossfire.

Doctor Catherine Smallwood, the WHO's Ebola incident manager, said the fighting has, quote, "already prevented teams from reaching some communities." End quote. She said it has also disrupted the tracking of people who were exposed, and left some treatment centres short of staff.

Here's why it matters. Ebola control depends on finding every contact of every case. When teams can't reach villages, the chain of tracing breaks, and confirmed counts start to lag behind the real spread. The AP piece did not say whether cases have spread beyond Congo, so I'll leave that as unknown.

Story two. Huawei convicted in Brooklyn.

On Tuesday, a federal jury in Brooklyn found the Chinese tech giant Huawei guilty on ten of twelve counts. TIME magazine reported the verdict on Wednesday. The convictions include bank fraud, wire fraud, conspiracy to steal trade secrets, money laundering, and violating US sanctions on Iran. Huawei subsidiaries were convicted alongside the parent company.

There were two important acquittals. The jury cleared Huawei of racketeering conspiracy, the most serious charge, and of conspiring to obstruct a grand jury investigation. TIME notes that the racketeering acquittal matters because a conviction could have led to the forfeiture of parts of the company. Huawei still faces fines, forfeiture and restitution. Sentencing is set for September twenty twenty-seven.

The two sides tell very different stories. A US prosecutor, Meredith Arfa, told jurors that, quote, "committing crimes to get ahead was ingrained in the very culture of Huawei." End quote. That's according to Anadolu Agency. Huawei's lawyer, Brian Heberlig, said prosecutors, quote, "began with guilt and worked backwards." End quote. He says the company will appeal.

Beijing responded on Wednesday. Foreign Ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun said, quote, "The Chinese government firmly opposes moves by the US suppressing and containing Chinese enterprises." End quote.

There's a Canadian thread here. The charges were announced in January twenty nineteen. Before that, in twenty eighteen, Canada had detained Huawei's chief financial officer, Meng Wanzhou, at Washington's request, which set off a diplomatic crisis. TIME reports her charges were dismissed in December twenty twenty-two.

Here's why it matters. The verdict landed just days after Donald Trump and Xi Jinping met in Shenzhen, as TIME points out. A conviction of China's flagship tech company is a new irritant in a relationship both leaders had been trying to steady.

Story three. Europe's budget summit ends in deadlock.

European Union leaders met in Brussels on Thursday for a special summit on the bloc's next long-term budget, covering twenty twenty-eight to twenty thirty-four. It ended early Friday morning, after nearly fifteen hours, with no deal. That's according to Euronews and the outlet EUalive.

The compromise on the table, from Council President António Costa, was one point seven eight trillion euros over seven years. Euronews reports that's about ten percent less than the original draft. It still wasn't enough for either side.

Seven so-called frugal countries, including Germany and the Netherlands, want deeper cuts. Seventeen countries calling themselves the Friends of Cohesion, which receive more regional funding, are pushing back against any further cuts.

Italy's Giorgia Meloni was blunt. Quote: "I will not sign under these conditions. We are light years away from a solution." End quote. That was reported by Anadolu Agency. Her complaint is that Italy would pay more into the budget and get less back. The Dutch prime minister, Rob Jetten, said, quote, "There's no need to hurry." End quote. But France's Emmanuel Macron warned that talks can't be reopened or concluded after December thirty-first. One diplomat told Euronews, quote, "It was a lot of political theatre." End quote.

Ukraine got squeezed. A proposed support loan of forty-five billion euros was overshadowed by the budget fight, Euronews reports. Al Jazeera reports that Bulgaria, the Czech Republic, Hungary and Slovakia oppose using EU funds for military aid to Ukraine. Germany's Friedrich Merz, meanwhile, has threatened to block a budget deal without a collective commitment to Ukraine.

Here's why it matters. The budget needs every one of the twenty-seven countries to agree. Leaders now try again at their regular December summit, and a string of national elections next year, including in France and Italy, will make compromise even harder.

And finally, one line to tie it together. All three stories this week are about systems straining under pressure: a health response blocked by gunfire, a trade relationship tested in a courtroom, and a union that can't agree on its own bills.
