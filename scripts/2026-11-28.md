## Full script
Good morning. It's Saturday, November twenty-eighth, and this is your Chronos digest. Today: fascinating facts.

Winter is close in Toronto, so we're looking at something that will be landing on your car soon. We'll cover why every flake has six sides, how the weather in a cloud decides its shape, the Vermont farmer who first photographed one, and why a snowy street sounds so quiet.

Today's theme: the snowflake.

Fact one. Six sides, written into the molecule.

Ask a child to draw a snowflake and they'll give it six arms. That's not just habit. According to SnowCrystals dot com, the research site run by Kenneth Libbrecht, a professor of physics at the California Institute of Technology, the symmetry starts at the smallest scale. Quote: "The six-fold symmetry you see in a snow crystal arises from the arrangement of water molecules in the ice crystal lattice." End quote.

In other words, the hexagon is already there before any arms grow. The water molecules lock together in a pattern with six-fold symmetry, and the visible crystal inherits it.

That leads to the old saying that no two snowflakes are alike. Libbrecht's site gives a simple reason it holds up. Quote: "since no two snow crystals follow the exact same path through the clouds as they fall, no two look exactly alike." End quote. Each flake takes its own trip through slightly different air, and that trip is recorded in its shape.

Fact two. The cloud decides the shape.

If the path matters, what in the path matters most? The answer is temperature, with humidity close behind. The same SnowCrystals site describes what's known as the snow crystal morphology diagram. It credits the discovery to the Japanese physicist Ukichiro Nakaya and his collaborators in the nineteen thirties.

The pattern is surprisingly fussy. Near minus two degrees Celsius, thin plate-like crystals form. Cool things down to around minus six, and the crystals grow as slender columns instead. Go colder again, to about minus fifteen, and large thin plates come back. Below that, you can find either plates or columns.

Humidity sets how fancy the result gets. When the air is drier and the crystals grow slowly, they stay simple. When the air is wet and they grow fast, you get the complex, branching shapes most of us picture.

So a few degrees can be the difference between a tiny ice needle and a classic star. A snowflake is a small record of the conditions it fell through.

Fact three. The Snowflake Man of Jericho.

Most of us know what a single snow crystal looks like because of one patient farmer in Vermont. According to Guinness World Records, the first photograph of an individual snow crystal was taken on January fifteenth, eighteen eighty-five, by Wilson Bentley, on his family's farm near Jericho, Vermont. Guinness says he used a glass-plate camera that could be mounted to a microscope.

The Linda Hall Library tells the rest of the story in a profile by its history of science consultant, William Ashworth. Bentley used a large bellows camera with a microscope lens. He caught falling snow on black velvet and recorded each image on a glass plate three by four inches across. Over his lifetime he built up more than five thousand three hundred plates of snowflakes.

In nineteen thirty-one, Bentley and William Humphreys published a book called Snow Crystals. The library says it held two hundred and twenty-seven plates, with roughly two thousand seven hundred images, about half of Bentley's life's work. Bentley died of pneumonia on December twenty-third of that year, at sixty-six, just as the book came out.

Fact four. Why snow is quiet, warm, and sometimes blue.

The last fact is about what happens once the flakes land. The National Snow and Ice Data Center explains that each ice crystal in a snow layer is surrounded by a lot of air. In its words, quote: "most of the total volume of a snow layer is made up of air." End quote.

All that trapped air has some odd effects. First, snow keeps things warm. The center says snow is, quote, "a good insulator, similar to the insulation in the ceiling of a house." End quote. It slows heat escaping from the ground to the cold air above.

Second, fresh snow is quiet. The center says sound waves are readily absorbed into the snow surface, dampening sound. That's the hush you notice on a street after a fresh snowfall. Once snow refreezes and hardens, it reflects sound instead, so noises can seem clearer and carry farther.

Third, there's colour. Snow usually looks white, but deep snow acts like a filter. The center says deep snow tends to absorb red light, which leaves the blue tints you sometimes see in a snowbank or a hole dug in deep snow. The center says that once light has travelled through about a metre of snow, it's mostly blue light that gets through, so the snow itself looks blue.

And one more number from the same source. Most snowflakes are less than one point three centimetres across. Bigger flakes, approaching five centimetres, can form, usually when temperatures are near freezing, winds are light, and the air is unstable. Because nobody routinely measures snowflake size, the true maximum isn't known.

And finally, one thing worth trying. The next time it snows in Toronto, take a dark piece of fabric or cardboard outside, let it cool, and catch a few flakes on it, the way Bentley did with black velvet. Your phone's camera, zoomed in close, should be enough to see the six-sided structure. Check the temperature too, and see whether you're getting plates, needles or stars.

That's your digest. It's Saturday, November twenty-eighth. Have a good one.
